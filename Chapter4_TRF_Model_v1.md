# Chapter 4. The Tri-Lateral Retention Feasibility Model and the Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation Mechanism

*Proposition statements and proofs in this chapter are the author's own. Regulatory claims are anchored to the primary instruments cited at each point.*

---

## 4.1 Purpose and Structure of the Chapter

This chapter contains the analytical core of the thesis. It does three things in sequence, and the sequence matters because each step licenses the next.

First, it establishes what the law actually requires to be retained, as distinct from what current practice retains. This is the necessity analysis begun in Chapter 2, Section 2.3, and it produces the decomposition on which the model rests.

Second, it constructs the Tri-Lateral Retention Feasibility (TRF) Model: a formal representation of the retention regimes as constraints over discretised time, together with two candidate semantics for what it means for an audit record to be verifiable. Within that model it states and proves four results. Proposition 1 establishes that under the semantics implicit in current practice the constraint system is infeasible. Proposition 2 establishes that under an alternative semantics the system is jointly satisfiable, and its proof is constructive: it exhibits the mechanism that achieves satisfaction. Proposition 3 states exactly when a deletion ceiling collides with a retention floor and what the ceiling's anchor decides, and Proposition 4 shows why moving a record out of the regulated environment, the first rival a reviewer would propose, meets the ceiling but not the right to erasure.

Third, it specifies that mechanism, Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation, in enough detail to be implemented, analyses the residue it leaves behind against the legal threshold that residue must clear, compares it against the rival designs a reviewer would propose instead, and maps it onto the Norwegian infrastructure verified in Chapter 2.

The order of exposition follows the order in which the thesis has developed the problem: the cross-border primary-use case is treated first throughout, with the Secure Processing Environment case following as the second and sharper instance.

A note on what the propositions are and are not. They are mathematical statements about a model, proved informally at the standard of rigour normal for design-science work, and checked executably in Chapter 5. They are not machine-checked, and they are not claims about the world independent of the model's fidelity. Their force depends entirely on whether the constraints faithfully translate the instruments, which is why Section 4.2 documents that translation claim by claim rather than asserting it.

## 4.2 What Must Be Retained: The Necessity Analysis

The decomposition that makes the model possible is not a modelling convenience. It is derived from the legal position established in Chapter 2, Section 2.3, and everything downstream inherits it.

Article 17(3)(b) GDPR exempts from erasure processing that is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation. The audit-retention obligations are such obligations. But the exemption is bounded by necessity, and Article 5(1)(c) requires that personal data be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the purposes of processing. The question is therefore not whether an audit record may be retained, but which parts of it necessity protects.

The purpose that the retention obligations serve is accountability: establishing, after the fact, that a particular access occurred, by whom, when, under what authority, and with what result. That purpose is served by the event metadata. It is not served by continued controller access to personal data the record carries beyond those attributes: the demographic content of the logged query, or, where a deployment keeps it, the content of the document that was exchanged. A supervisory authority investigating whether a Spanish clinician's access to a Norwegian patient summary in March 2027 was lawful needs to know that the access occurred, which clinician performed it, under which eIDAS assertion, against which consent policy, and whether it succeeded. It does not need the patient's name and birth date as typed into the query, still less the patient's medication list, to answer that question.

This yields the central claim of the necessity analysis: *the accountability function of a cross-border audit record is discharged by its metadata and by a binding commitment to its payload, and does not require the payload to remain accessible.* Retention of accessible plaintext payload for ten years therefore exceeds what Article 17(3)(b) protects. Article 5(1)(c) positively requires that it not be retained where the purpose can be achieved without it.

Two sources support it and one limits it. The Norwegian Data Protection Authority's internal-control guidance places exactly this weighing on the controller, requiring determination of lawful retention periods and deletion deadlines through explicit balancing of purpose, business need, and both retention rights and retention duties (Datatilsynet, n.d.-a). The Authority's privacy-by-design guidance equates anonymisation with deletion (Datatilsynet, n.d.-b), which is what permits a transformed record to satisfy an erasure obligation at all. The limit is that the same Authority sets a high threshold for when data cease to be personal: encryption protects data rather than rendering them unidentifiable, and data remain personal while any key or linkage capable of re-identification is retained (Datatilsynet, 2015). The residue left by the mechanism must therefore be analysed against that threshold rather than assumed to clear it, which is the work of Section 4.9.

## 4.3 The Model: Objects and Notation

Let the time domain be discretised into months, indexed by $t \in \mathbb{N}$, with $t = 0$ the month in which the system begins operation. Monthly granularity is chosen because every obligation in the system is expressed in months or years, and because the shortest deadline in the system, the erasure response period, is one month.

Let $A$ be the set of audit records. Each record $a \in A$ is generated by a single access event and is represented as a tuple

$$
a = \big(\, m(a),\; p(a),\; c(a),\; k(a),\; t(a),\; F(a),\; \sigma(a) \,\big)
$$

where:

- $m(a)$ is the *event metadata*: the actor identifier, the actor's national contact point, the patient pseudonym, the document category accessed, the legal basis and consent policy applied, the timestamp, and the outcome. Metadata contains no clinical content. It is partitioned as $m(a) = m_{acc}(a) \cup m_{subj}(a)$. The *accessor fields* $m_{acc}(a)$ record who acted, when, under which legal basis or permit, and with what outcome. The *subject fields* $m_{subj}(a)$ relate the event to a data subject: the patient pseudonym and any reference to that subject's records. The partition does work only in the Secure Processing Environment case (Section 4.6).
- $p(a)$ is the *personal-data payload*: the personal data the record carries beyond what accountability requires (Section 4.2). Its content depends on the record class. For a cross-border record it is the identifying content of the logged query. The IHE Cross Gateway Patient Discovery transaction (ITI-55), by which one country's gateway locates a patient in another, requires the person's name and birth time unless an identifier is supplied, and requires both gateways to record the query segment, base64-encoded, in the audit message's ParticipantObjectQuery field (IHE International, 2024, Sections 3.55.4.1.2.1 and 3.55.5.1). The audit record therefore holds the patient's demographic query and not only a pseudonym. For a Secure Processing Environment record it is the content of the logged activity where the environment logs at that level: the query text and any output retained with the entry. The model does not require $p(a)$ to be a clinical document, only personal data that the accountability purpose does not need.
- $c(a)$ is a *commitment* to the payload, $c(a)$ = Commit($p(a), r(a)$), where $r(a)$ is a per-record random salt. The commitment scheme is required to be *hiding* (the commitment reveals nothing about the payload to an adversary without the salt) and *binding* (no second payload can be opened against the same commitment). The requirement that it be hiding rather than a bare hash is a consequence of Section 4.9 and is not optional.
- $k(a)$ is the *payload key*: the key material under which $p(a)$ is stored.
- $t(a)$ is the month in which the access event occurred.
- $F(a) \in \mathbb{N}$ is the *retention floor* applying to the record, expressed in months.
- $\sigma(a) \in \{\text{primary}, \text{secondary}\}$ is the *pathway* in which the record arose.

The floor is a parameter of the record rather than a constant of the model. The form is taken from chain-of-custody practice in physical cross-border logistics, where a consignment carries one custody record whose retention parameter is set by whichever jurisdiction's regime governs it, rather than a separate ledger per jurisdiction; Chapter 2, Section 2.7 sets out the correspondence. In the present setting it takes two values:

- $F(a) = 120$ for cross-border audit records held in ATNA-compliant audit repositories, this being the ten-year minimum reported as the eHDSI deployment baseline for MyHealth@EU operations (Simjanoska Misheva et al., 2025). The value is illustrative: the primary agreement inspected requires audit trails without stating a period (eHealth Network, 2017, clause II.4.2), and by Corollary 2.2 no result depends on it. The IHE ATNA profile itself prescribes no retention period; the floor arises from the deployment binding, and the model is correct only if this distinction is preserved.
- $F(a) = 12$ for access and activity logs of Secure Processing Environments, this being the one-year minimum imposed by Article 73(1)(e) of Regulation (EU) 2025/327 (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). The Joint Action's specification recommends five years (TEHDAS2, 2026, Table 5.4, OPR-18, p. 51); by Proposition 3 a longer floor enlarges the set of records whose permit expiry falls inside it, and by Corollary 2.2 no result depends on the value.

Two event processes act on records. An *erasure request* arrives for record $a$ at month $t_r(a)$ if the data subject exercises Article 17 in respect of the personal data underlying that record; if no request arrives, $t_r(a) = \infty$. A *permit expiry* occurs at month $t_\pi(a)$ for records with $\sigma(a)$ = secondary, being the month in which the data permit under which the processing occurred expires; for primary-pathway records $t_\pi(a) = \infty$.

Finally, let $\alpha(a,t) \in \{0, 1\}$ be the *payload-accessibility variable*: $\alpha(a,t) = 1$ if the controller can recover $p(a)$ in intelligible form at month $t$, and 0 otherwise. This single binary variable per record per month is the entire state space of the model, and its minimality is deliberate: the conflict turns on exactly one question, which is whether the payload is readable, and a formulation that admits no other state variable cannot obscure that question behind modelling detail. The cost of that minimality is that the model cannot represent field-level redaction, where some elements of a payload are erased and others retained; Chapter 3, Section 3.7 records this as a stated abstraction.

The Secure Processing Environment case needs one further variable, and only on the reading of Article 68(12) that Section 4.6 identifies. Let $\beta(a,t) \in \{0, 1\}$ be the *subject-link variable*: $\beta(a,t) = 1$ if the subject fields $m_{subj}(a)$ can be read in the environment at month $t$, and 0 otherwise. Where that reading does not apply, $\beta$ is unconstrained and plays no part in any result.

Let $V(a,t) \in \{0, 1\}$ denote whether record $a$ *counts as verifiable* at month $t$. $V$ is not defined yet; defining it is the subject of Section 4.5, and the two competing definitions are what the propositions distinguish.

## 4.4 The Three Constraints

Each regime is now expressed as a constraint on the trajectory of $\alpha$ and $V$.

*C1: Retention floor.* For every record $a$ and every month $t$ with $t(a) \le t \le t(a) + F(a)$:

$$
V(a,t) = 1 \qquad \forall\, t \in \big[\, t(a),\; t(a) + F(a) \,\big]
$$

The record must count as verifiable throughout its retention window. The window is closed at both ends and so spans $F + 1$ monthly indices. This is deliberate: the obligations are minimums ("at least one year"), and a record written during month $t(a)$ completes $F$ full months only during month $t(a) + F$, so requiring verifiability through that month is the conservative reading of "at least". A half-open window would shorten every obligation by up to a month. Source: eHDSI deployment baseline for $F = 120$; Article 73(1)(e) EHDS for $F = 12$.

*C2: Erasure right.* For every record $a$ for which a valid erasure request arrives at $t_r(a) < \infty$, and for every month $t \ge t_r(a) + \delta$:

$$
\alpha(a,t) = 0 \qquad \forall\, t \ge t_r(a) + \delta
$$

The payload must cease to be accessible within the response period $\delta$. Source: Article 17(1) GDPR, with $\delta$ determined by Article 12(3) GDPR, which requires the controller to act without undue delay and in any event within one month of receipt, extendable by two further months for complex or numerous requests provided the data subject is informed within the first month. The model takes $\delta = 1$ as the baseline and admits $\delta = 3$ as the extended case; results are stated for arbitrary finite $\delta$ and do not depend on which is chosen. Constraint C2 is triggered only by a *valid* request. The model does not represent the Article 17(3) exemptions under which a controller may lawfully refuse, and so treats every arriving request as valid; Chapter 3, Section 3.7 explains why this affects the frequencies reported in Chapter 5 but not the propositions.

*C3: Deletion ceiling.* For every record $a$ with $\sigma(a)$ = secondary and every month $t \ge t_\pi(a) + 6$:

$$
\alpha(a,t) = 0 \qquad \forall\, t \ge t_\pi(a) + 6
$$

and, where the record's subject fields are electronic health data within the environment (Section 4.6),

$$
\beta(a,t) = 0 \qquad \forall\, t \ge t_\pi(a) + 6
$$

Electronic health data must not remain in the Secure Processing Environment more than six months after permit expiry. Source: Article 68(12) EHDS, which provides that "the electronic health data within the secure processing environment shall be deleted within six months of the expiry of the data permit" (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025), as operationalised by TEHDAS2 (2025).

The structural asymmetry between C1 on one hand and C2–C3 on the other is the whole problem. C1 is a lower bound on the persistence of a property; C2 and C3 are upper bounds on the persistence of accessibility. Whether they conflict depends entirely on whether $V$ is defined in terms of $\alpha$, which is the question Section 4.5 makes explicit.

## 4.5 Two Semantics of Verifiability

*Semantics I: plaintext verifiability.* A record counts as verifiable exactly when its payload is accessible:

$$
V^{I}(a,t) = \alpha(a,t)
$$

This is the semantics implicit in the design of audit systems that predate the erasure right. Under it, an audit record whose payload cannot be produced is treated as an incomplete or broken record, and audit integrity is understood as the continued producibility of what was accessed. No instrument in the system states this semantics; it is inherited from practice, which is precisely why it has not been examined.

*Semantics II: accountability-preserving verifiability.* A record counts as verifiable when its accountability content is intact and any loss of payload accessibility is itself accounted for:

$$
V^{II}(a,t) = 1 \iff \text{(i)} \wedge \text{(ii)} \wedge \text{(iii)}
$$

where, at month $t$: (i) $m_{acc}(a)$ is intact and unmodified, and so is $m_{subj}(a)$ unless its removal is recorded under (iii); (ii) $c(a)$ is present and binding; and (iii) if $\alpha(a,t') = 1$ for some $t' < t$ and $\alpha(a,t) = 0$, or likewise for $\beta$, then an invalidation entry $\iota(a)$ recording the transition, its month, its legal ground, and its authorising actor, is present in the audit trail and is itself covered by (i).

Under Semantics II an audit record that has lost its payload is not a broken record. It is a complete record of an access event *and* of the lawful invalidation of that event's payload. The audit trail grows rather than shrinks when erasure occurs.

Neither semantics is stipulated by the instruments. Section 4.10 argues that Semantics II is the better reading of what the instruments require; the propositions below establish what follows from each.

## 4.6 Proposition 1: Infeasibility under Plaintext Verifiability

> *Proposition 1.* Under Semantics I, the constraint system $\{C1, C2, C3\}$ is infeasible for any record $a$ such that
>
> $$
> t_r(a) + \delta \le t(a) + F(a) \quad\text{or}\quad t_\pi(a) + 6 \le t(a) + F(a).
> $$

*Proof.* Take a record $a$ satisfying the first disjunct, and let $t^{*} = t_r(a) + \delta$. By hypothesis $t^{*} \le t(a) + F(a)$, and since erasure requests arrive after the access that generates the record, $t^{*} \ge t(a)$. Hence $t^{*}$ lies within the retention window, so C1 requires $V(a,t^{*}) = 1$. Under Semantics I, $V^{I}(a,t^{*}) = \alpha(a,t^{*})$, so C1 requires $\alpha(a,t^{*}) = 1$. But C2 requires $\alpha(a,t) = 0$ for all $t \ge t_r(a) + \delta = t^{*}$, so C2 requires $\alpha(a,t^{*}) = 0$. The two requirements are contradictory, and no assignment of $\alpha$ satisfies both. The argument for the second disjunct is identical with $t^{*} = t_\pi(a) + 6$ and C3 in place of C2. ∎

*Corollary 1.1 (cross-border case).* For cross-border audit records, $F(a) = 120$. The system is infeasible for record $a$ whenever an erasure request arrives at any month $t_r(a) \le t(a) + 120$ − $\delta$: that is, at any point in the first nine years and eleven months of a ten-year window under the baseline response period. The condition is therefore not one of unusual timing: it holds for a request arriving in any but the last month of the window. How often data subjects exercise Article 17 is an empirical question that the model does not answer (Chapter 3, Section 3.7).

*Corollary 1.2 (Secure Processing Environment case).* For Secure Processing Environment access logs, $F(a) = 12$ and $\sigma(a)$ = secondary. Suppose the permit expires at or before the sixth month of the record's retention window, so that $t_\pi(a) + 6 \le t(a) + 12$. Then C1 and C3 are contradictory at $t^{*} = t_\pi(a) + 6$ by the argument above, provided C3 reaches the record. *Where it does, no erasure request is required.* It does so when the log entry is itself electronic health data within the environment, which, on the reading set out below, is the case for logs kept at patient-record granularity and not for logs kept at dataset granularity. For such records the infeasibility follows from two provisions of a single regulation acting on a single environment. Article 73(1)(e) requires the identifiable log to remain verifiable for twelve months, and Article 68(12) requires the electronic health data within the environment to be deleted six months after permit expiry. Where the remaining permit duration at the time the log is written is six months or less, the two windows necessarily overlap. The model makes no claim about how often this occurs in practice; the collision's existence, not its frequency, is what Corollary 1.2 asserts.

Where it holds, Corollary 1.2 is the sharpest form of the thesis's problem statement. It shows that the conflict does not depend on the exercise of a data-subject right, on the eHDSI deployment baseline, or on any synthesis across instruments of differing legal weight. It is internal to Regulation (EU) 2025/327.

*The interpretive condition on which Corollary 1.2 depends.* The corollary holds if, and only if, the deletion obligation of Article 68(12) reaches the identifiable access logs that Article 73(1)(e) requires to be kept. That condition is contestable and is stated as such.

Article 68(12) provides that "the electronic health data within the secure processing environment shall be deleted within six months of the expiry of the data permit". Its scope is set by location rather than by provenance: it does not say "the data made available under the data permit". Its object, however, is electronic health data, which Article 2(2)(a) and (c) define as "data concerning health and genetic data, processed in an electronic form", personal or non-personal. An access log is therefore not reached merely because it sits within the environment. An entry recording that an authorised researcher opened a dataset at a given time concerns the researcher, not anyone's health, and Article 73(1) ties the identifiability of logs to the persons who access: access is restricted to "authorised natural persons listed in the data permit" (point (a)), and the handling of data to "authorised identifiable individuals" (point (c)). The Joint Action's specification for these environments reads Article 73(1)(e) the same way: "All SPE logs MUST identify the actor" (TEHDAS2, 2026, Table 5.1, EHDSR-7, p. 31). An entry kept at patient-record granularity is different. Recording that a named patient's record in a disease cohort was accessed reveals that the patient belongs to the cohort, which is data concerning health. Such entries are electronic health data within the environment, and on the broad reading, which takes the provision's scope from its location, Article 68(12) reaches them, and Corollary 1.2 follows for them.

The contextual case for the narrow reading is that the provision sits within the data-permit article, adjacent to provisions on permit duration, extension, and storage fees, and that the mischief it addresses is the indefinite retention of permitted datasets after the purpose that justified them has lapsed. The rest of the paragraph supports this: it concerns the cost of storing the dataset, "a storage system with reduced capabilities", and "the formula for the creation of the requested dataset". On that reading the obligation attaches to the permit dataset, the access logs fall outside it, and no collision between Articles 68(12) and 73(1)(e) arises.

This thesis states Corollary 1.2 conditionally, for logs kept at patient-record granularity on the broad reading, and gives the grounds for both readings rather than choosing one by assertion. Little turns on which reading prevails. Under the narrow reading, Corollary 1.2 lapses, and with it the claim that infeasibility arises without any data-subject action. Proposition 1 itself is untouched, because its first disjunct concerns erasure requests rather than permit expiry: identifiable access logs remain personal data whoever provided them, they remain within the scope of Article 17, and a request arriving inside the twelve-month floor collides with Article 73(1)(e) exactly as the cross-border case collides with the eHDSI floor. Corollary 1.1 is untouched entirely. Proposition 2, the mechanism, and the residue analysis are untouched, since they are indifferent to which instrument generates the deadline.

The narrow reading, or logs kept at dataset granularity, therefore cost the thesis its sharpest instance and none of its results. The broad reading, for logs kept at patient-record granularity, adds a collision that requires no data subject to act, and Proposition 2 resolves it without choosing between the readings, because the accessor fields that Article 73(1)(e) requires to be identifiable are not the subject fields that Article 68(12) may reach (Section 4.7). Chapter 6 treats the resolution of this interpretive question as a matter for supervisory authority rather than for this author, alongside the authority question concerning verifiability itself.

*Where the ceiling is anchored.* Corollary 1.2 and the cross-sector instance of Chapter 6, Section 6.9a are both cases of one condition, the structural content of the problem rather than a property of either instrument.

> *Proposition 3 (anchoring).* Let a record's floor of $F$ months run from its creation month $t(a)$, and let a ceiling of $L$ months run from an event at month $e(a) \ge t(a)$. Under Semantics I, the floor and the ceiling are jointly satisfiable for $a$ if and only if $e(a) + L > t(a) + F$. Hence (i) if the ceiling is anchored to the same event as the floor, $e(a) = t(a)$, they are satisfiable for every record if and only if $L > F$, a condition decidable from the text alone; and (ii) if the ceiling is anchored to an external event, then whenever $L \le F$ they fail for exactly those records with $e(a) - t(a) \le F - L$, a set that the text does not reveal and each record's history determines.

*Proof.* C1 under Semantics I requires $\alpha(a,t) = 1$ for every month $t$ up to $t(a) + F$; the ceiling requires $\alpha(a,t) = 0$ for every $t \ge e(a) + L$. Since $e(a) + L \ge t(a)$, some month of the window carries both demands if and only if $e(a) + L \le t(a) + F$. Cases (i) and (ii) substitute $e(a) = t(a)$ and rearrange. ∎

The proposition corrects as well as formalises. A collision needs a ceiling no longer than the floor whichever way it is anchored; what the anchor decides is whether the collision can be seen. A provision that measured both limits from one event and set the ceiling inside the floor would contradict itself on its face. Section 17 of politiregisterloven, a floor of twelve months and a ceiling of thirty-six from the same log entry, does not (Chapter 6, Section 6.9a). A ceiling measured from an external event can sit inside the floor with no visible contradiction, because whether the two clocks cross depends on when that event falls for each record. Article 68(12) is of the second kind: six months from permit expiry against twelve months from the log entry, with the collision arising for exactly the records whose permit expires within six months of the entry, which is the condition of Corollary 1.2. The diagnostic question for any candidate instance is therefore whether a ceiling no longer than the floor is anchored to an event other than the one the floor runs from.

## 4.7 Proposition 2: Feasibility under Accountability-Preserving Verifiability

> *Proposition 2.* Under Semantics II, the constraint system $\{C1, C2, C3\}$ is jointly satisfiable for every record $a \in A$, for every arrival pattern of erasure requests $\{t_r(a)\}$ and permit expiries $\{t_\pi(a)\}$, and for every floor parameter $F(a) \in \mathbb{N}$.

*Proof (constructive).* Define the following trajectory for an arbitrary record $a$. Let

$$
t^{I}(a) = \min\big(\, t_r(a) + \delta,\;\; t_\pi(a) + 6 \,\big)
$$

be the *invalidation month*, with the convention min($\infty, \infty) = \infty$. Construct the trajectory:

1. For $t < t^{I}(a)$: set $\alpha(a,t) = 1$. Retain $m(a), c(a), k(a)$, and $p(a)$.
2. At $t = t^{I}(a)$, if finite: destroy $k(a)$ irreversibly; where the ground is Article 68(12) and C3 reaches the subject fields, remove $m_{subj}(a)$ as well and set $\beta(a,t) = 0$; append to the audit trail an invalidation entry $\iota(a)$ recording the month, the legal ground (Article 17(1) or Article 68(12) as applicable), what was removed, and the authorising actor; retain $m_{acc}(a)$ and $c(a)$ unchanged.
3. For $t > t^{I}(a)$: set $\alpha(a,t) = 0$, and $\beta(a,t) = 0$ where step 2 removed the subject fields. Retain $m_{acc}(a), c(a)$, and $\iota(a)$.
4. If C3 reaches the subject fields and $t^{I}(a) < t_\pi(a) + 6$, so that an erasure request fixed the invalidation month, remove $m_{subj}(a)$ at $t_\pi(a) + 6$, set $\beta(a,t) = 0$ from that month, and append a second entry to $\iota(a)$ recording it.

It must be shown that this trajectory satisfies all three constraints.

*C2 is satisfied.* If $t_r(a) < \infty$ then $t^{I}(a) \le t_r(a) + \delta$ by construction, and by steps 2–3, $\alpha(a,t) = 0$ for all $t \ge t^{I}(a)$, hence for all $t \ge t_r(a) + \delta$. If $t_r(a) = \infty$, C2 is vacuous.

*C3 is satisfied.* If $\sigma(a)$ = secondary and $t_\pi(a) < \infty$ then $t^{I}(a) \le t_\pi(a) + 6$, and by the same steps $\alpha(a,t) = 0$ for all $t \ge t_\pi(a) + 6$, and $\beta(a,t) = 0$ from the same month wherever C3 reaches the subject fields. If $t^{I}(a)$ was set by an earlier erasure request, step 4 removes the subject fields at $t_\pi(a) + 6$. Otherwise C3 is vacuous.

*C1 is satisfied.* Let $t$ be any month in the retention window, $t(a) \le t \le t(a) + F(a)$. Condition (i) holds because no step of the construction modifies or removes $m_{acc}(a)$, and the only removal of $m_{subj}(a)$ is recorded in $\iota(a)$. Condition (ii) holds because no step removes $c(a)$; the commitment is not derived from $k(a)$ and its persistence is unaffected by key destruction. For condition (iii): if $\alpha(a,t) = 1$ the condition is vacuous; if $\alpha(a,t) = 0$ then $t \ge t^{I}(a)$, and step 2 has placed $\iota(a)$ in the audit trail, where step 3 retains it, and $\iota(a)$ is covered by (i). Hence $V^{II}(a,t) = 1$ throughout the window. The terminal transition of Section 4.8 takes place after the window and is not part of this trajectory.

All three constraints are satisfied, for arbitrary arrival times and arbitrary $F(a)$. ∎

*Corollary 2.1.* The construction requires no coordination between the erasure process and the retention process, and no knowledge at the time a record is created of whether an erasure request will ever arrive. The invalidation month is determined entirely by events as they occur.

*Corollary 2.2.* Because $F(a)$ is arbitrary in the proof, the result holds simultaneously for the ten-year cross-border floor and the one-year Secure Processing Environment floor, and for any national floor substituted for either. The model is tri-lateral in constraint type and indifferent to the number of floor instances.

*A note on what kind of result this is.* The results of this chapter are stated as propositions rather than propositions, because their proofs are short once the semantics are fixed. The weight of the argument lies elsewhere: in Section 4.2's necessity analysis, which justifies Semantics II, and in Propositions 3 and 4, which say where the conflict arises and where cryptography is necessary. One objection is that Proposition 2 proves little, because Semantics II was defined in full knowledge of the trajectory that satisfies it. The objection has force and the response is to concede its premise and deny its conclusion. The mathematical content of Proposition 2 is indeed modest: once verifiability is decoupled from payload accessibility, the existence of a satisfying trajectory is close to immediate, and the proof is a verification rather than a discovery. But the substantive claim of this chapter was never that a satisfying trajectory exists under some semantics; any number of semantics would admit one, including trivial semantics under which every record counts as verifiable always. The claim is that *this* semantics is a defensible reading of what the retention obligations require, grounded in the necessity boundary of Article 17(3)(b), the minimisation principle of Article 5(1)(c), and the accountability purpose the obligations serve. It is also the weakest departure from current practice that achieves feasibility, preserving every element of the audit record that the accountability purpose needs and discarding only what it does not. Proposition 1 carries the load that Proposition 2 does not: it establishes that *some* departure is unavoidable, because the status quo admits no solution at all. Proposition 2 then shows that a principled and minimal departure suffices. The legal defensibility of Semantics II is argued in Section 4.2 and its want of authority is confronted in Chapter 6, Section 6.2; it is not established by the proposition, and no reader should take the proposition as establishing it.

*What the proposition does and does not establish.* It establishes that the constraint system as formalised admits a solution, and exhibits one. It does not establish that the exhibited solution is lawful (that depends on whether the residue it leaves clears the legal threshold, which Section 4.9 addresses) nor that key destruction can be verified by an external party, which Section 4.10 concedes it cannot. The invalidation entry specified in Section 4.8 is an *attested* record of destruction, not a *proof* of it; the proof-of-secure-erasure and certified-deletion literatures reviewed in Chapter 2, Section 2.4 mark that boundary, and Chapter 6 treats it as the outer limit of the contribution. Proposition 2 is a mathematical result about a model, and its value is that it converts a question that has been debated qualitatively into one with a definite answer under stated assumptions.

## 4.8 The Mechanism: Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation

The trajectory constructed in the proof of Proposition 2 is the specification of the mechanism. This section states it as an implementable design.

*Key hierarchy.* Each record's payload is encrypted under a per-record data key $k(a)$. Per-record rather than per-subject or per-dataset granularity is required because the invalidation events of Proposition 2 occur at record granularity: a permit expiry invalidates the records of one permit, while an erasure request invalidates the records relating to one data subject, and these sets intersect without nesting. Data keys are themselves held under a key-encrypting key in a hardware security module or equivalent custody boundary. Destruction of $k(a)$ is effected by destruction of the wrapped data key and any cached copies, within the documented custody boundary. This is the hierarchy that key-management guidance prescribes, in which data keys are protected by key-encrypting keys and destruction means removing "all traces of a cryptographic key so that it cannot be recovered by either physical or electronic means" (Barker, 2020, Section 2.1). The optional module of the artefact executes it: per-record 256-bit data keys under AES in Galois/Counter Mode (Dworkin, 2007), each wrapped under a key-encrypting key and destroyed at the record's invalidation month (Appendix A, A.9e). The custody object stands in for a hardware security module, so destruction there is destruction in program memory.

*Ciphertext.* At $t^{I}(a)$ the ciphertext of $p(a)$ is deleted from the audit store together with $k(a)$. Nothing in Semantics II uses it once the key is gone, and deleting it removes the exposure that a retained ciphertext would carry to future cryptanalytic capability, quantum capability included, over the rest of a ten-year floor (Section 4.9). Key destruction remains the operative erasure for copies the store cannot delete at once, such as replicas and backups: they become unreadable at $t^{I}(a)$ and leave by ordinary rotation. The specification for Secure Processing Environments accepts exactly this: electronic health data "MUST be deleted or rendered unrecoverable within six months of permit expiry, including any backups or redundant copies" (TEHDAS2, 2026, Table 5.2, OPR-6, p. 43). The same requirement continues: deletion must proceed "while ensuring compliance with applicable legal obligations and traceability or reproducibility requirements where relevant", and its rationale leaves it to "the deletion and retention policy" to "balance these obligations". The specification asks for the balance and does not say how it is struck; for the logs, this mechanism is one way to strike it.

*Commitment.* $c(a)$ = Commit($p(a), r(a)$) with a per-record salt $r(a)$. The salt is stored with the metadata until invalidation and is destroyed together with $k(a)$ at $t^{I}(a)$. Before invalidation it lets an auditor open the commitment against the payload. After invalidation no opening can be checked, because the payload is no longer readable, while a retained salt would let anyone holding a candidate payload confirm a guess against $c(a)$ (Section 4.9). Destroying the salt does not weaken binding, which is a property of the commitment value: no second payload can be opened against $c(a)$, and after $t^{I}(a)$ no payload can be opened against it at all. A party that holds its own copy of the payload and must later prove that it corresponds to $c(a)$ has to obtain and keep the opening before invalidation; the audit store does not keep one for it. The scheme must be hiding: an adversary holding $c(a)$ and lacking $r(a)$ must learn nothing about $p(a)$. A bare unsalted hash of the payload does not satisfy this and must not be used, for the reason given in Section 4.9.

*The invalidation registry.* Invalidation entries are appended to the audit trail as first-class records. Each entry $\iota(a)$ contains the identifier of the invalidated record, the invalidation month, the legal ground, the authorising actor, and a commitment to the destroyed key's identifier: not to the key itself. The registry is append-only. Invalidation is an event that is logged, not an event that erases the log. Key-management guidance anticipates exactly this record: once a key is destroyed, "its previous existence may be recorded (e.g., in metadata or audit logs)" (Barker, 2020, Section 7.6). The invalidation entry is that record, given a legal ground and an authorising actor.

*Tamper evidence.* An append-only registry is only as good as the means of detecting an append-only violation. Secure audit logging has addressed this since Schneier and Kelsey (1999), whose hash-chained logs make earlier entries unalterable without detection; Merkle trees (Merkle, 1988) let the same assurance be checked for a single entry with a logarithmic proof (Crosby & Wallach, 2009), and Certificate Transparency standardised that construction for public logs (Laurie, Langley, & Kasper, 2013). The mechanism appends every access event, invalidation and subject-link removal to such a log, with the leaf and node hashing of RFC 6962, and Semantics II condition (i) is checked as an inclusion proof against the log's root rather than as the presence of fields (Chapter 5, Section 5.6). What enters a leaf is chosen so that the log never needs erasing: the accessor fields, the creation month and the commitment, never the payload and never the subject fields. The tension that Chapter 2, Section 2.4 found unresolved in blockchain audit logging, an immutable trail facing an erasure right, therefore does not arise: what the right and the ceiling reach is held outside the log and destroyed there, and what the log holds is what accountability requires.

*Accumulator-based validity witnesses.* The set of currently valid (non-invalidated) records is maintained in a dynamic cryptographic accumulator supporting efficient revocation (Camenisch & Lysyanskaya, 2002). This provides an auditor with a succinct proof that a given record either is or is not currently valid, without the auditor enumerating the registry. The accumulator is an efficiency and verifiability layer, not a load-bearing component of the propositions: Proposition 2 holds if validity is established by linear scan of the registry, and the accumulator makes that check succinct. The accumulator is specified but not implemented in the artefact (Section 6.1). Dynamic accumulators of the Camenisch and Lysyanskaya type also require a trusted accumulator manager holding a trapdoor, which adds a trust assumption stated in Section 4.9.

*The auditor's view.* After invalidation, an external auditor examining record $a$ can establish four things. The access event occurred, with all its accountability attributes, which follows from $m(a)$. A specific payload was bound to that event, which follows from $c(a)$. The payload was invalidated, and the auditor can see when, on what legal ground, and by whom, from $\iota(a)$. And the record's current validity status is as claimed, which follows from the accumulator witness where that layer is used, and otherwise from a scan of the registry. Each of these entries is, moreover, in the log as first written, which follows from its inclusion proof against the published root. What the auditor cannot do is read the payload. This is the intended state, and it is the difference between this mechanism and the practitioner crypto-shredding pattern reviewed in Chapter 2, Section 2.4: that pattern retains ciphertext and asserts that erasure occurred, whereas this mechanism produces a verifiable record of what was invalidated and under what authority.

*End of life at floor expiry.* The constraints of Section 4.4 govern the record's behaviour through its retention window, but the window ends, and the model would be incomplete if it said nothing about what follows. After $t = t(a) + F(a)$, the last month of the closed window on which C1 is defined, the retention obligation lapses, and with it the necessity that justified retaining $m(a)$ under Article 17(3)(b). Because the metadata contains the patient pseudonym and the actor identifier, it is personal data, and the storage-limitation principle of Article 5(1)(e) GDPR requires that it not be kept in identifiable form longer than the purpose demands. The mechanism therefore specifies a terminal transition. In the first month after the window, $t(a) + F(a) + 1$, the record undergoes a second invalidation, in which the identifying fields of $m(a)$ are destroyed, together with $k(a)$, the salt $r(a)$ and the ciphertext where no earlier invalidation removed them. What remains is a non-identifiable residue, being the event type, the document category, the outcome, and the commitment, which is sufficient for statistical and assurance purposes and insufficient to relate the record to any data subject. The terminal transition is itself logged, and the record's final state is the only state in the mechanism that is intended to be anonymous rather than pseudonymous in the sense of Section 4.9. Where a longer-running obligation applies to the same record (a national archival duty, or litigation hold) the terminal transition is deferred and the floor parameter $F(a)$ is set to the longer period, which is the parameterisation the model already supports.

*Applying the mechanism to the two cases.* For the cross-border primary-use case, the mechanism operates at the audit repository of the National Contact Point, at the point where the ATNA audit record is written. For the Secure Processing Environment case, it operates at the environment's access-logging boundary. The identifiable log required by Article 73(1)(e) is written with its activity content committed rather than retained in plaintext beyond the ceiling, and with its subject fields held apart from its accessor fields. At $t_\pi(a) + 6$ the payload key is destroyed and, for logs kept at patient-record granularity, the subject fields are removed. The environment then holds a log that identifies every person who accessed it, when and under which permit, as Article 73(1)(e) requires, and holds no electronic health data from that log, as Article 68(12) requires on the broad reading. Whether auditing "all processing operations" after the ceiling also needs the subject link is an interpretive question this thesis cannot settle; Chapter 7, Section 7.4 puts it to the supervisory authority.

## 4.9 Residue Analysis

Proposition 2 leaves a residue: $m(a), c(a), \iota(a)$, the leaf hashes of the audit log and, where the optional layer is used, the accumulator state. The salt $r(a)$, the key $k(a)$ and the ciphertext of $p(a)$ are destroyed at invalidation (Section 4.8). Whether the mechanism is lawful depends on whether this residue sits on the correct side of the threshold at which data cease to be personal. This section performs that analysis, and it is included because Chapter 2 identified its absence as the fourth gap in the literature.

*The threat.* Finck's analysis for the European Parliament establishes that hashed or encrypted data likely remain personal data under GDPR so long as re-identification remains possible (Finck, 2019; Finck, 2018). Applied to this mechanism, the threat is direct: if $c(a)$ is an unsalted hash of the payload, then an adversary who can guess candidate payloads can confirm a guess by recomputing the hash. For clinical documents drawn from a constrained space (a diagnosis code, a medication from a formulary) the guessing space may be small enough to make this practical. An unsalted commitment would therefore be a surviving item of personal data, and the mechanism would have relocated the erasure problem rather than solved it.

*The response.* This is why the hiding property is a requirement of the design and not an implementation preference. With a per-record salt $r(a)$ of adequate length, confirming a guessed payload requires the salt; the commitment alone leaks nothing. Because the salt is destroyed with the key, no party can confirm a guessed payload against $c(a)$ after invalidation unless it kept its own opening beforehand. What has been destroyed is both the ability to *recover* the payload, which depended on $k(a)$, and the ability to confirm a guess about it from the audit store, which depended on $r(a)$.

The audit log raises the same threat one level up, and the same response answers it. A leaf hash must outlive the record's fields, since the root depends on it, and it is computed over the accessor fields and the commitment. After the terminal transition the commitment survives and the accessor fields do not; an adversary who can enumerate candidate accessors, a small set in any one environment, could then recompute the leaf and confirm which accessor it was. The specification therefore places a per-entry random nonce in every leaf, held with the record and destroyed at the terminal transition with the identifying fields, so that a surviving leaf hash confirms nothing. The artefact does not implement the terminal transition, and its leaves omit the nonce accordingly (Section 6.1).

*The limit.* The residue therefore does not meet the strict Norwegian anonymisation threshold. Datatilsynet's guidance holds that encryption protects rather than anonymises, and that data remain personal while any key or linkage capable of re-identification is retained (Datatilsynet, 2015). By that standard the residue, which keeps metadata identifying the access event, is best characterised as pseudonymised rather than anonymous until the terminal transition. This thesis does not claim otherwise, and any claim that the mechanism achieves anonymisation would be false.

*Why the mechanism is nonetheless defensible.* The applicable legal test is not anonymisation but erasure. The European Data Protection Board's Guidelines 02/2025 accept that where physical deletion is not technically possible, deleting the decryption key can render data unintelligible and so serve the erasure obligation. They maintain at the same time that encrypted and hashed data may remain personal data, and that the unintelligibility holds at least until the algorithm is broken (European Data Protection Board, 2026b). The requirement that the controller document the threat model under which the data are considered unrecoverable is not the Board's formulation. It is Aikebaier's (2026) interpretation, which connects a Destruction-IND security notion to the Board's criterion, and it is adopted here as a design discipline rather than asserted as a regulatory requirement. The final version 2.0, adopted on 7 July 2026 after public consultation, sharpens rather than softens the constraint this places on the present mechanism: it confirms that both metadata and content may in principle constitute personal data, and that this may extend to encrypted and hashed data. The residue characterisation of this section is therefore the one the Board's final position requires, and a thesis claiming anonymisation for that residue would now be contradicted by the guidance it relies upon. Aikebaier's (2026) systematisation formalises this correspondence, establishing an equivalence proposition between a Destruction-IND security notion and the Board's unrecoverability criterion. The design consequence is that the mechanism's lawfulness rests on a documented threat model, which must state: the custody boundary within which key destruction occurs; the assumed adversary's access to that boundary; the leakage assumptions for the hardware holding key material; and the algorithmic-break horizon over which the ciphertext's confidentiality is assumed to hold. A mechanism deployed without that documentation would not satisfy the Board's condition however sound its cryptography.

*The trust assumptions.* Because key destruction is attested rather than proven, the mechanism's guarantees rest on a set of assumptions that a deploying institution must be willing to accept, and naming them is part of the documentation the Board's condition requires.

The controller is trusted to execute destruction when the invalidation entry says it did, and to have had no copy of the key outside the custody boundary at that moment. The custody boundary itself, whether a hardware security module or an equivalent key-management service, is trusted not to retain recoverable key material after a destruction instruction, and not to leak it through side channels during its lifetime. The audit-trail operator is trusted not to remove or alter entries, which the append-only construction makes detectable but not impossible: an altered entry fails its inclusion proof, and a rewritten history is exposed by a consistency proof between an earlier root and the current one, provided earlier roots are published or countersigned (Laurie, Langley, & Kasper, 2013). The artefact implements inclusion proofs only. Where the optional accumulator layer is used, its manager is also trusted, since a party holding the accumulator trapdoor can forge validity witnesses. And the cryptographic assumption is that the commitment scheme remains binding and hiding, and the payload cipher remains unbroken, across the retention horizon.

What an external auditor can verify without any of this trust is bounded. The auditor can verify that the metadata is internally consistent, that an invalidation entry exists with its month, ground and authorising actor, and that the entry is bound into the append-only trail. The auditor can also verify that a commitment survives, to which a payload could be opened if one were produced. What the auditor cannot verify is that the key is gone. That gap is the difference between attestation and proof, and it closes only with hardware-backed or cryptographically certified deletion, which Chapter 6, Section 6.3 treats as the boundary of this contribution.

*The residual cryptographic risk.* Over a ten-year floor, the algorithmic-break horizon is a live concern rather than a formality: anything retained for a decade must be assumed to face cryptanalytic capability that does not exist when it is written. Deleting the ciphertext at invalidation (Section 4.8) removes this exposure for the store's own copy, so what is retained long-term is metadata, the commitment and the invalidation entry. The risk is reduced, not eliminated. Copies the store cannot delete at once, such as backups, hold ciphertext until rotation removes them; a record with no invalidation event keeps its ciphertext until the terminal transition; and the commitment must stay hiding across the horizon, which is a requirement on the hash function rather than on a cipher. This is recorded as a limitation in Chapter 6 rather than resolved here.

## 4.10 Rival Designs

Six alternative designs are considered. Five are rejected for stated reasons; the sixth is accepted for the ceiling alone and bounded by Proposition 4.

*Rival 1: Rely on the Article 17(3)(b) exemption and retain in full.* This is the dissolution argument answered in Chapter 2, Section 2.3 and Section 4.2 above. It fails on the necessity boundary: the exemption protects retention that the legal obligation requires, and Section 4.2 established that the accountability purpose does not require payload accessibility. It fails additionally and decisively in the Secure Processing Environment case, where Article 68(12) admits no exemption parallel to Article 17(3)(b), so that Corollary 1.2's infeasibility survives the argument entirely.

*Rival 2: Anonymise the audit record instead.* If the record were genuinely anonymised, Datatilsynet's equation of anonymisation with deletion would discharge the erasure obligation (Datatilsynet, n.d.-b). But genuine anonymisation of a cross-border audit record destroys its accountability function: an audit trail that cannot identify which clinician accessed which patient's record is not an audit trail, and Article 73(1)(e) requires the logs to be *identifiable* in terms. Anonymisation satisfies C2 and C3 by violating C1.

*Rival 3: Minimise the payload at capture, so that nothing needs erasing.* Recording less at the outset is good practice and is compatible with this mechanism; where a deployment can minimise, the mechanism has less to invalidate. For cross-border records it does not dissolve the problem, because the payload is put there by the standard rather than by the deployment: the IHE patient-discovery transaction requires the audit message to carry the query segment, name and birth time included (Section 4.3). A deployment cannot minimise it away without departing from the profile. In the Secure Processing Environment case minimisation is a genuine alternative for the ceiling: a log kept at dataset granularity carries no subject fields and falls outside Article 68(12) on either reading, at the cost of patient-level traceability. The specification requires logs to identify the actor and to cover "access to electronic health data", and requires logging to be "proportionate, risk-based, and technically feasible" and to "avoid unnecessary collection of detailed user behaviour not required for auditability or misuse detection" (TEHDAS2, 2026, Table 5.4, OPR-17, p. 51). It sets no requirement on the granularity of logs, which leaves the choice, and with it Corollary 1.2, to the operator. Minimisation does not remove from the scope of Article 17 the payload the standard requires, which is the case Proposition 4 below shows no relocation or reduction of the log can escape.

*Rival 4: Delete the record and log the deletion.* This is the closest rival and deserves the most care. Deleting the audit record while logging that a deletion occurred satisfies C2 and C3, and preserves a trace. It fails C1 under both semantics: under Semantics I trivially, and under Semantics II because condition (ii) fails: no commitment survives, so the auditor can establish that *something* was deleted but not that the deleted item was the payload bound to that specific access event. The distinction matters in adversarial conditions. A controller who deletes records and logs deletions can conceal the substitution of one record for another; a controller bound to a surviving commitment cannot. The mechanism's advantage over Rival 4 is precisely the verifiability that Chapter 2 identified as absent from the practitioner crypto-shredding pattern.

*Rival 5: Retain recoverable encrypted copies until the later date.* This is the resolution developed in the closest prior art, the DePaul retention-and-purging programme reviewed in Chapter 2, Section 2.4. It holds encrypted copies in shadow tables and backups, with each copy's key selected to expire on the governing purge date, and effects purging by destroying that key (Scope, Rasin, Lenard, Heart, & Wagner, 2022; Scope & Rasin, 2025). Where both obligations are floors and the purge date is fixed in advance, this is the right design.

It cannot work against a ceiling, and the reason is the residue analysis of Section 4.9 applied in reverse. A copy whose key still exists is recoverable, and recoverable ciphertext held together with the means of decryption remains personal data under the threshold set out in Chapter 2, Section 2.3. Retaining such copies to satisfy the Article 73(1)(e) floor is therefore exactly what the Article 68(12) ceiling forbids of the environment holding them. The prior art satisfies a retention obligation by preserving recoverability; the mechanism specified here satisfies one by destroying recoverability and preserving accountability instead. That inversion, rather than any difference in cryptographic technique, is what the tri-lateral case requires.

*Rival 6: Move the logs out of the environment at permit closure.* If Article 68(12) is read by location, exporting the access logs at permit closure to an audit store outside the environment, held for instance by the health data access body, satisfies it literally without cryptography, on the premise that Article 73(1)(e) fixes how long the logs are kept and not where. The rival is correct about the ceiling, and this thesis accepts it as a sufficient answer to Corollary 1.2. It does not answer the erasure right, for a reason that is general enough to state as a result.

> *Proposition 4 (location and right).* Let a ceiling be anchored to a location, requiring data to be absent from that location after a deadline, and let a right be anchored to the data subject, requiring data to be inaccessible to any controller holding them after a deadline. Under Semantics I, relocating a record before the ceiling's deadline satisfies the ceiling and the floor together, while the floor and the right remain infeasible for every record satisfying the first disjunct of Proposition 1.

*Proof.* The ceiling quantifies over the location: after relocation the record is not in it, so the ceiling holds, and on the rival's premise the floor is met at the new location. The right quantifies over the controller: Article 17(1) obliges the controller holding the data to erase them, and a controller receiving the record receives the obligation with it. Relocation therefore leaves C1 and C2 as they were, and the argument of Proposition 1 applies unchanged. ∎

Two consequences follow. Cryptographic invalidation is necessary for the request-driven conflict, which no relocation escapes, and optional for the location-anchored one, where relocation is a lawful alternative. And relocation is not free. The exported log still carries its subject fields, now held by a second controller for the rest of the floor, so the personal data have been moved rather than reduced; where those fields are electronic health data, they are held outside the measures Article 73(1) prescribes for such data. The partition of Section 4.7 meets the ceiling without moving anything, and does so with the same mechanism that meets the right.

*The critique that is not a rival.* SecuPi (2025) argues that cryptographic erasure introduces operational complexity in row-level encryption scenarios and that auditable key management adds rather than removes overhead. This is not an alternative design for the problem; it is a cost objection, and it is partly correct. Per-record key granularity in a high-volume audit repository is an operational burden. The response is that the burden is the price of a solution to a problem that has none otherwise, and that the burden falls on audit records rather than on clinical data stores, which is the smaller of the two surfaces. Chapter 6 engages this critique at length rather than dismissing it.

## 4.11 Integration with the Norwegian Infrastructure

The mechanism is specified against the Norwegian infrastructure as verified in Chapter 2, Section 2.6, not against an idealisation of it.

*Primary use: the National Contact Point.* Norway's NCPeH is in live pilot operation (Helsedirektoratet, 2026), as Chapter 2, Section 2.2 describes. The mechanism attaches at the sixth step of the OpenNCP transmission flow, where the ATNA audit record is written to the audit repository. It requires no change to service discovery, patient discovery, document retrieval, pivot translation, or the security and consent module. This placement is deliberate: it is the narrowest point of the flow at which the intervention achieves its purpose, and interventions at the other five steps would touch the interoperability surface that Member States have agreed and Norway has implemented.

*Secondary use: Helsedataservice and the Secure Processing Environments.* Helsedataservice holds decision authority for eleven central health registries (Folkehelseinstituttet, 2024). The mechanism attaches at the access-logging boundary of the Secure Processing Environment rather than inside the environment's analytical machinery. This placement is forced by the empirical finding that the Norwegian environment landscape is fragmented well beyond the three NORTRE nodes, extending to controller-provided servers, folders, and in some cases consumer cloud services and disconnected personal computers (SPUHiN, 2024). A mechanism requiring capabilities of the environment would fail against that reality; a mechanism operating on the log the environment must in any case produce does not. The reference architecture of the Joint Action's specification locates the same boundary (TEHDAS2, 2026, Figure 5.13, p. 60). It divides a Secure Processing Environment into a control-and-management layer, comprising user authentication and identification, activity monitoring and access control, and the project workspaces in which data users work. The mechanism belongs to the activity-monitoring component, which writes the logs, and takes each permit's expiry from the coordination portal of the health data access body, which issues the permit. It touches neither the project workspace nor the interfaces to data users and data holders.

*Compatibility with the national criteria.* The Norwegian criteria for Secure Processing Environments, in the version put to hearing, require an information-security management system as mandatory, recommend ISO 27001 certification, and require eID-based authentication at defined assurance levels (SPUHiN, 2025). The mechanism requires nothing beyond these: key custody within a documented boundary is an information-security-management concern of exactly the kind the mandatory requirement already covers. It adds no requirement that the criteria do not already impose, which was the design constraint derived from Chapter 2's third gap.

*Relation to the pending implementing acts.* The binding technical requirements for Secure Processing Environments are to be laid down by Commission implementing acts under Article 73(5) EHDS, due by 26 March 2027 (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025), after the submission of this thesis. The secondary-use provisions they will operationalise become applicable on 26 March 2029 under Article 105. The specification published by TEHDAS2 during 2026 as deliverable D7.4 is the best available anticipation of their content (TEHDAS2, 2026). The mechanism is therefore specified against a moving target, and the appropriate claim is compatibility with the infrastructure as currently specified rather than compliance with requirements not yet adopted. Chapter 6 treats the implications of this timing for the thesis's policy relevance.

## 4.12 Summary

This chapter formalised the tri-lateral retention conflict as a constraint-satisfaction problem over a single binary accessibility variable per record, with the retention floor parameterised so that the ten-year cross-border audit floor and the one-year Secure Processing Environment log floor are instances of one constraint. It proved that under the plaintext-verifiability semantics implicit in current practice the system is infeasible for any cross-border record whose erasure request arrives inside its window and, for Secure Processing Environment logs kept at patient-record granularity on the broad reading, without any erasure request at all, where two retention provisions of a single regulation collide over a single environment. It proved that under an accountability-preserving semantics the system is jointly satisfiable for every arrival pattern and every floor, and the proof exhibited the trajectory that achieves satisfaction. It showed that a ceiling collides with a floor only when it is no longer than the floor, and that the ceiling's anchor decides whether the collision can be read from the text (Proposition 3), and that relocating a record meets the ceiling but not the right (Proposition 4). That trajectory, specified as Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation, retains metadata and a hiding commitment, and destroys the payload key at the earlier of the erasure deadline and the deletion ceiling. It appends the invalidation to a tamper-evident audit log as a first-class, witnessed event, in a log built never to hold what erasure reaches. The salt and the payload ciphertext go with the key. At floor expiry it performs a terminal transition that destroys the identifying metadata, leaving a non-identifiable statistical residue, so that the record's whole life cycle, not merely its retention window, is accounted for under the storage-limitation principle.

The mechanism's residue is pseudonymised rather than anonymous by the strict Norwegian standard, it is defensible under the erasure test the European Data Protection Board has articulated provided the threat model is documented, and its long-horizon cryptographic risk is real and recorded. The limitations bearing on this chapter, the informal proof standard and the conditionality on honest key destruction among them, are those stated canonically in Chapter 3, Section 3.7 and are not restated here. Six rival designs were considered: five were rejected on stated grounds and one, relocation, was accepted for the ceiling alone. The principal cost objection was acknowledged rather than dismissed. The integration analysis placed the mechanism at the two narrowest points of the Norwegian infrastructure at which it achieves its purpose, requiring nothing of the substrate that the national criteria do not already require.

Chapter 5 demonstrates both propositions executably, over synthetic records generated as described in Chapter 3.
