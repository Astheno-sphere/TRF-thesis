The thesis develops the Tri-Lateral Retention Feasibility (TRF) Model, a constraint-satisfaction formulation in which the semantics of audit verifiability is an explicit parameter. Under plaintext-verifiability semantics the constraints are infeasible whenever an erasure or deletion deadline falls inside a retention window (Proposition 1). Under accountability-preserving semantics they are jointly satisfiable for every arrival pattern of erasure requests and permit expiries and for every retention floor (Proposition 2). Two further results locate the conflict: a ceiling collides with a floor only if it is no longer than the floor, and the event the ceiling is anchored to decides whether the collision is visible in the text or only in each record’s history (Proposition 3); moving a record out of the regulated environment satisfies the ceiling but not the right to erasure (Proposition 4). The witnessing mechanism, Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation, [-destroys a per-record payload key and removes the record’s link to the patient, while retaining-]{+destroys a per-record payload key and, for Secure Processing Environment logs at the deletion ceiling, removes the record’s link to the patient, while retaining+} the accessor’s identity, event metadata, a hiding commitment and a logged invalidation entry in a tamper-evident log.

Cryptographic operations use standard library primitives: salted commitments constructed with a standard hash function, and simulated key destruction implemented as irreversible removal of key material from the sandbox’s state. [-The commitments are hiding by construction, which is-]{+The commitments are hiding by construction when salts are drawn at random (the reported run derives salts from the record identifier for reproducibility; see Appendix A, A.6), which is+} a design requirement rather than an implementation detail, since Chapter 2 established that an unsalted hash of personal data may itself remain personal data. Every access event, invalidation and subject-link removal is appended to an append-only Merkle log, so that the verifiability condition can be checked as an inclusion proof that fails if an entry is altered. No zero-knowledge proof system is implemented at any point, and none is required. The mechanism’s verifiability rests on commitments, on the recorded invalidation event and on the log, not on succinct arguments. The optional module repeats the record lifecycle with real AES-256-GCM envelope encryption, to show that unreadability follows from key destruction rather than being asserted.

Rival 4: Delete the record and log the deletion. This is the closest rival and deserves the most care. Deleting the audit record while logging that a deletion occurred satisfies C2 and C3, and preserves a trace. It fails C1 under both semantics: under Semantics I trivially, and under Semantics II because condition (ii) fails: no commitment survives, so the auditor can establish that something was deleted but not that the deleted item was the payload bound to that specific access event. The distinction matters in adversarial conditions. A controller who deletes records and logs deletions [-can conceal the substitution of one record for another; a controller bound to a surviving commitment cannot.-]{+can conceal the substitution of one record for another. A commitment bound into the log at write time constrains this only for a party that kept its own opening; once the salt and payload are destroyed no auditor can open it, so the advantage over this rival is narrower than first stated.+} The mechanism’s advantage over Rival 4 is precisely the verifiability that Chapter 2 identified as absent from the practitioner crypto-shredding pattern.

Proof. The ceiling quantifies over the location: after relocation the record is not in it, so the ceiling holds, and on the rival’s premise the floor is met at the new location. The right quantifies over the controller: Article 17(1) obliges the controller holding the data to erase them, [-and a controller receiving the record receives the obligation with it-]{+and, as a legal premise rather than a mathematical consequence, a controller receiving the record receives the obligation with it+}. Relocation therefore leaves C1 and C2 as they were, and the argument of Proposition 1 applies unchanged. ∎

This is the state the mechanism is designed to produce, and it is worth reading against what Semantics I would have demanded. The access event is fully established: which researcher, under which permit, with what outcome. The link to the patient was removed at the ceiling, and its removal is itself a logged entry. The binding to a specific payload survives in the commitment. The invalidation is itself a logged, grounded, attributed event rather than a silent absence. And the payload is unreadable, at month nineteen as at month sixteen. Feasibility results with an executable witness. Proposition 1 establishes infeasibility under plaintext-verifiability semantics; Proposition 2 establishes satisfiability under the alternative and exhibits the trajectory that achieves it. Proposition 3 gives the condition under which a ceiling and a floor collide, which the cross-sector test of Chapter 6, Section 6.10 [-confirmed on a statute outside health-]{+illustrated on a statute outside health (an instance generated by the thesis’s own code, which checks the code rather than independently confirming the proposition)+}; Proposition 4 shows why relocation answers the ceiling and not the right. Both were demonstrated over two hundred records, producing ninety infeasible records and ninety-five violations under the first semantics and none under the second, with a separately written exhaustive search confirming that under the first semantics no assignment of payload accessibility whatsoever succeeds. The proofs are informal and the demonstration covers generated instances; neither is presented as more.

Selective disclosure of invalidation completeness. The mechanism creates a problem it does not solve. When a data subject exercises Article 17, every record relating to them is invalidated, and an auditor should be able to confirm that all of them were. But identifying which records those are reveals the subject’s cross-border care history, which is precisely what the erasure was meant to protect. Proving completeness of invalidation without revealing the invalidated set is a zero-knowledge problem, and it corresponds to the fourth of the five open problems identified by Aikebaier (2026), composition with zero-knowledge selective disclosure. This is where succinct arguments become necessary rather than decorative, and the audit log of Chapter 4, Section 4.8 gives the problem a concrete form. If each leaf also carried a hiding commitment to its record’s subject link, a circuit taking the published root as its public input could prove that every leaf committed to a given subject is matched by an invalidation leaf, while revealing neither the leaves nor their number. Pairing-based arguments give proofs of constant size (Groth, 2016), and universal-setup systems avoid a trusted setup per circuit (Gabizon, Williamson, & Ciobotaru, 2019); which suits a regulator who must verify without trusting the controller is a design question for that work.
