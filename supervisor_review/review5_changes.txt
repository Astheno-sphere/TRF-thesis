OLD: The most direct legal objection to the existence of the conflict must be addressed before its technical treatment. Article 17(3)(b) GDPR provides that the right to erasure does not apply to the extent that processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject. On a broad reading, the audit-retention obligation of the eHDSI baseline would simply disapply Article 17 for audit records, and the three regimes would never collide. This thesis argues that the exemption narrows the conflict without dissolving it, for three reasons grounded in the primary instruments. First, the exemption is bounded by necessity: it excuses only the processing that the legal obligation actually requires, and the storage-limitation and data-minimisation principles of Article 5(1)(c) and 5(1)(e) GDPR continue to apply to whatever is retained. The accountability function of a cross-border audit record, namely who accessed what, when, from where, under which legal basis, and with what outcome, does not require continued controller access to the full personal-data payload of the underlying exchange. Retention of the payload in accessible plaintext for the length of the floor therefore exceeds the necessity that Article 17(3)(b) protects. Second, the Norwegian Data Protection Authority’s internal-control guidance places precisely this balancing on the controller. It requires organisations to determine lawful retention periods and deletion deadlines through an explicit weighing of purpose, business need, and both retention rights and retention duties imposed by other law (Datatilsynet, n.d.-a). That formulation presupposes, rather than dissolves, the coexistence of retention obligations and deletion obligations on the same records.
