## 2.1 Review Protocol

### 2.1.1 Type of review and why it is named as such

This review is a structured narrative review conducted under a documented and dated protocol. It is not a systematic review. A systematic review executes a pre-registered search strategy in a single sweep, against a fixed set of indexed databases, with independent screening by more than one reviewer. This review proceeded iteratively across four dated search rounds and a fifth, verification-only round, used natural-language rather than Boolean queries, drew substantially on primary regulatory and government sources that are not indexed in bibliographic databases at all, and was screened by one reviewer. Calling it systematic would misdescribe it.

What the review can offer instead is replicability of a different and, for this subject, more appropriate kind. It provides a complete record of what was searched and when, the criteria applied, and the screening account. It also provides a prospective Boolean protocol that a researcher may execute against indexed databases, to test whether the conclusions survive a systematic sweep. Section 2.1.6 provides that protocol and states plainly that it was not the instrument used here.

### 2.1.2 What the review had to establish

The review is organised around the conceptual architecture of the thesis rather than around exhaustive coverage of a field. It had to establish four conditions on which the contribution depends. The first is that the cross-border landscape under the European Health Data Space is advanced enough, and Norwegian participation committed enough, for the contribution to be timely. The second is that the audit-retention versus erasure tension is real, named in primary sources, and unresolved. The third is that cryptographic and formal approaches have matured enough to make the proposed mechanism plausible while leaving the specific conflict unaddressed. The fourth is that the Norwegian secondary-use infrastructure provides a concrete substrate. A search strategy optimised for recall across a defined corpus would not have served these conditions, three of which turn on primary legal and governmental material.

### 2.1.3 Sources searched

Academic literature was searched through Google Scholar, arXiv, the Cryptology ePrint Archive, PubMed and PubMed Central, and publisher platforms including Springer, IEEE Xplore, MDPI, Sage, JMIR, and BMC. Primary regulatory material was retrieved directly from EUR-Lex, the European Commission's Directorate-General for Health and Food Safety, and the European Data Protection Board. Standards material was retrieved from IHE International, NIST, and HL7 and HL7 Norway. Norwegian governmental and institutional material was retrieved from helsedirektoratet.no, helsedata.no, datatilsynet.no, nortre.no, and stortinget.no. Joint Action material was retrieved from tehdas.eu. Patent literature was searched through public patent databases in the final round.

### 2.1.5 Selection criteria and screening

Two criteria governed inclusion throughout. Every cited source must be retrievable through a public URL, a DOI, or a primary repository at the time of writing, so that any claim can be independently verified. And where secondary commentary conflicts with primary regulatory or standards text, the primary text prevails; secondary sources are cited for analysis or for evidence of practice, never for the factual content of an instrument.

Material was included in three categories. The first is peer-reviewed work published between 2018 and 2026 bearing on cross-border health data exchange, cryptographic erasure, formal compliance verification, or the GDPR erasure right. The second is primary regulatory, legislative, standards, or governmental material of any date. The third is practitioner and industry material, where the claim being supported is one about industry practice rather than about law.

Four kinds of material were excluded. The first is work addressing data-protection regimes outside the European Union and European Economic Area without comparative bearing, and the second is opinion writing without an identifiable evidential basis. The third is work not retrievable at the time of writing, and the fourth is any source that could not be verified against a primary or publisher record.

Screening proceeded from query results through title and abstract assessment to full-text or full-document assessment, with sources entering the working bibliography only after the verification step. Because the review was iterative rather than a single sweep, a PRISMA flow diagram reporting identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion counts cannot be reconstructed for rounds 1 and 2, whose intermediate counts were not retained. Reporting reconstructed counts would give the appearance of a systematic process that was not followed. What is retained and reported instead is the full query log, the working bibliography with its verification status per source, and the correction record.

### 2.1.7 Limitations of the protocol

Four limitations follow from how the review was conducted.

The exploratory phase before round 1 is not claimed as evidence, because its queries were never recorded. Screening was performed by a single reviewer, so no inter-rater reliability can be reported. Intermediate screening counts for the first two rounds were not retained, so no PRISMA flow diagram is offered. The queries were natural-language, so recall cannot be characterised in the way a Boolean strategy over an indexed corpus permits. The absence of prior work on the central conflict, reported in Section 2.9, rests on four dated search rounds, two of them adversarial, together with an independent corroborating statement in the cryptographic literature. But an absence established by searching remains weaker evidence than a positive finding. Section 2.9 therefore states the additional reasons the gap is likely genuine, rather than resting on the searches alone.

## 2.2 The Cross-Border Health Data Exchange Landscape

The European Union's cross-border health data infrastructure rests on a regulatory framework that has matured rapidly during the past three years, and on a technical platform that has been in operation, in voluntary form, for more than a decade. The two layers are now converging under the European Health Data Space Regulation, which establishes the legal architecture within which the existing technical platform must scale.

Regulation (EU) 2025/327 of 11 February 2025 establishing the European Health Data Space was published in the Official Journal on 5 March 2025 and entered into force on 26 March 2025. Its entry into force opened a multi-year transition during which Member States and European Economic Area countries must align their national infrastructures with binding Union-wide requirements (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). Entry into force and application must be distinguished, and the distinction bears directly on this thesis. Under Article 105 the Regulation applies generally from 26 March 2027, with staggered commencement thereafter. The secondary-use provisions of Chapter IV, which include both the Article 68(12) deletion ceiling and the Article 73(1)(e) log-retention floor, become applicable on 26 March 2029, and further milestones fall in March 2031 and March 2035. The regulation governs both primary use, meaning the use of electronic health data for the provision of healthcare to the data subject, and secondary use, meaning use for research, innovation, policy-making, regulatory activities, and public health (European Commission, 2025a). For primary use, the regulation establishes a calendar that the implementing acts must operationalise by 26 March 2027. Group One priority categories of patient summaries and ePrescriptions become exchangeable across all Member States by 26 March 2029, and Group Two categories of laboratory results, medical imaging, and hospital discharge reports follow by 26 March 2031 (Healthy Europe, 2026; European Commission, 2025a). Each Member State must designate one or more digital health authorities and inform the Commission of them by 26 March 2027 (Article 19(1)), and the requirements on electronic health record systems follow the same Article 105 schedule, applying from 26 March 2029 to the systems that process the first priority categories (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025).

The technical platform implementing cross-border primary use is MyHealth@EU, the operational successor to the eHealth Digital Service Infrastructure (eHDSI). MyHealth@EU connects national systems through National Contact Points for eHealth (NCPeH), each operated by the participating country's competent authority and funded historically through the Connecting Europe Facility Telecom Programme (2015–2020) and currently through the EU4Health Programme (2021–2027) (European Commission, 2025b). Governance is exercised by two coordinating bodies: the eHealth DSI EU countries Expert Group (eHMSEG), composed of managers responsible for NCPeH implementation in participating countries, and the eHealth Operational Management Board (eHOMB), composed of European Commission representatives and the eHMSEG Chairs (European Commission, 2025b).

The MyHealth@EU operational architecture follows a six-step transmission flow at the NCPeH gateway level, recently characterised in detail by Simjanoska Misheva et al. (2025). When a healthcare provider in a country of treatment requests cross-border data, the source country NCPeH proceeds through six steps. It performs service discovery to locate the patient's home country gateway, and patient discovery against the patient's national identifier. It then retrieves the requested document, typically an International Patient Summary or an ePrescription, in the source country's native clinical document format. A pivot transformer converts that national clinical document representation to the common European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format, using SNOMED CT, LOINC, and other terminology bindings. The security and consent module then validates the requesting clinician's eIDAS identity token and applies XACML-based consent policies. Finally, the transaction is logged in an IHE Audit Trail and Node Authentication compliant audit repository, whose retention period is set by the deployment binding (Section 2.3).

Norway's integration with MyHealth@EU is being implemented through a project that began in January 2023 under the joint operational responsibility of Helsedirektoratet and Norsk Helsenett SF, the national operator of the closed Norwegian health network, with a planned duration of 41 months. The pilot is live at the emergency clinics in Bodø and Stjørdal, connected to Portugal, Czechia, and Finland for the receipt of patient summaries concerning citizens of those states requiring care in Norway. The Bodø and Stjørdal clinics are in production with Czechia and Portugal, and the emergency clinics in Trondheim and Ålesund, operating on Helseplattformen, have requested the service for the next stage (Helsedirektoratet, 2026). In 2026 the project began its next stage, establishing NCP-A, the contact point through which Norwegian health data will be shared with other participating states (Helsedirektoratet, 2026). The legal basis, privacy notices and data protection impact assessment were completed by 31 March 2025. The Directorate's portfolio reporting records that no relevant patients had yet presented for the solution to be used in practice, so the infrastructure is operational while the volume of records generated remains small; this thesis relies on the former and does not claim the latter. Infrastructure for receiving ePrescriptions has been planned and developed but is not yet in use, and Norway's scenario of implementing ePrescription without eDispensation was carried as a change proposal to the requirement catalogue and approved in the eHealth Operational Management Board on 12 March (Helsedirektoratet, 2026). Because Norway is a member of the European Economic Area rather than of the European Union, the EHDS Regulation does not apply directly but is being processed for incorporation through the EEA Joint Committee. The Norwegian Directorate of Health delivered its formal EHDS impact and gap analysis on 11 April 2025, identifying the principal areas in which Norwegian law and infrastructure will require alignment (Helsedirektoratet, n.d.).

Parallel to the primary-use track, the regulation establishes a secondary-use track through which national Health Data Access Bodies coordinate access to electronic health data for research, innovation, and policy purposes. The interconnecting infrastructure for secondary use is HealthData@EU, which complements MyHealth@EU and operates under different technical and governance arrangements. The technical, functional, and security specifications for the Secure Processing Environments through which secondary-use data must be processed have been developed under the TEHDAS2 Joint Action, a coordinated effort of twenty-nine countries running until December 2026 (Healthy Europe, 2026). TEHDAS2 published these specifications during 2026. They set out the minimum technical, functional, and security capabilities that Secure Processing Environments must offer, together with baseline interoperability requirements for federations of such environments, and position the document as input to standardisation work and to the European Commission's implementing acts (TEHDAS2, 2026). The implementing acts themselves, which will lay down the binding technical, organisational, information-security, confidentiality, data-protection, and interoperability requirements for Secure Processing Environments under Article 73(5) EHDS, are due by 26 March 2027 and had not been adopted at the time of writing. The national body exercising access-decision authority, and the presumptive Health Data Access Body under this regime, is Helsedataservice, hosted at the Folkehelseinstituttet (Norwegian Institute of Public Health), which since 2023 has held the decision-making authority for eleven statutory national health registries (Folkehelseinstituttet, 2024).

The historical context of these institutional arrangements is important for the present thesis. The Norwegian central analytics platform Helseanalyseplattformen was paused in December 2021 following legal challenges related to the Schrems II judgment on transatlantic data transfers, and was formally avviklet by the Ministry of Health and Care Services in February 2023. The Direktoratet for e-helse, which had led much of the early national e-health standards work, was itself dissolved in May 2023, with its core functions absorbed into Helsedirektoratet from 2024 onwards (Helsedirektoratet, 2024). The Norwegian secondary-use infrastructure that the EHDS will be implemented against is therefore not a centralised analytic platform. It is a distributed model resting on the three university-based trusted research environments, TSD at the University of Oslo, SAFE at the University of Bergen, and HUNT Cloud at NTNU, coordinated through the NORTRE collaboration (Norwegian Trusted Research Environments, n.d.).

The cross-border landscape is therefore mature in its regulatory architecture, advancing in its technical infrastructure, and concretely defined in its Norwegian implementation. It is against this landscape that the audit-retention and erasure tensions become not abstract but operationally consequential, as Section 2.3 will demonstrate.

## 2.3 The Audit-Retention versus Right-to-Erasure Tension

The thesis is built around a specific structural tension between three retention regimes that operate concurrently on the same cross-border health data event. Each regime is grounded in primary sources: a technical integration profile, a binding regulation, and an evolving Joint Action specification. None resolves the others. This section examines each regime in turn, then characterises the resulting conflict, the principal legal objection to its existence, and the regulatory doctrine that has developed around what counts as erasure.

The first regime is technical. The IHE Audit Trail and Node Authentication (ATNA) profile, maintained in Volume 1 Chapter 9 of the IHE IT Infrastructure Technical Framework, specifies the foundational audit and security requirements for nodes participating in interoperable health information exchange (IHE International, 2023). ATNA establishes a Basic Secure Node that must authenticate other nodes, encrypt communications using TLS, log triggered events such as queries, views, additions, deletions, and changes within the security domain, and transmit those audit records to an Audit Record Repository. The IHE specification itself does not mandate a single global retention period, leaving that decision to the realm-specific or domain-specific deployment binding. The eHDSI security framework that operationalises ATNA within the MyHealth@EU infrastructure is reported to specify retention in concrete terms: Simjanoska Misheva et al. (2025) characterise a minimum of ten years for audit repositories as the binding deployment baseline for cross-border NCPeH operations. The primary document this thesis could inspect, the agreement under which National Contact Points join the cross-border services, requires each party to ensure its contact point's compliance "with the requirements for logs, audit trails, non-repudiation" and states no period in its main text (eHealth Network, 2017, clause II.4.2); the annexes that would fix one were not available. The thesis therefore treats ten years as an illustrative value of the floor parameter rather than as an established requirement, and Chapter 4, Corollary 2.2 shows that no result depends on it. This retention floor exists because cross-border audit records support not only patient-facing access logs but also accountability for the eIDAS-based authentication of the requesting clinician, the consent validation step, and the audit trail required under the European Health Data Space patient-rights provisions.

The second regime is legal. Article 17 of the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) establishes the data subject's right to obtain from the controller the erasure of personal data concerning them without undue delay, where one of the conditions in Article 17(1) applies (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016). These conditions include the cessation of the original purpose, the withdrawal of consent without an alternative legal basis, an objection under Article 21 with no overriding legitimate interest, and unlawful processing. The data controller bears an independent obligation to erase in these conditions, irrespective of whether the data subject has formally exercised the right (European Data Protection Board, 2026a). Article 17(3) provides limited exemptions, of which two are directly relevant to the cross-border audit context. Article 17(3)(b) permits the controller to refuse erasure where processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation imposed by Union or Member State law. Article 17(3)(d) permits refusal where processing is necessary for scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes, in so far as erasure is likely to render impossible or seriously impair the achievement of those purposes. The European Data Protection Board's Coordinated Enforcement Framework report on Article 17 was published on 18 February 2026, following an investigation involving 32 supervisory authorities and 764 controllers. It identified the implementation of retention periods among the recurring issues, and recommended further practical guidance on how to define and implement retention periods consistent with national legal obligations (European Data Protection Board, 2026a; Matheson, 2026).

The third regime is operational and emerging. The TEHDAS2 Joint Action's guideline for Health Data Access Bodies on procedures and formats for data access specifies a deletion deadline. Electronic health data must be deleted from a Secure Processing Environment within six months of the expiry of the data permit, under Article 68(12) of the European Health Data Space Regulation (TEHDAS2, 2025; European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). This is a ceiling rather than a floor. After the six-month window, only anonymised analytical results may continue to exist outside the Secure Processing Environment, and the permitted data are not to remain within it. The same TEHDAS2 deliverable specifies a five-phase user journey for data access that ends in a deletion event marking the closure of the permitted processing.

The regulation places a retention floor on that same environment. Article 73(1)(e) EHDS requires of Secure Processing Environments "the keeping of identifiable logs of access to and activities in the secure processing environment for the period necessary to verify and audit all processing operations in that environment; logs of access shall be kept for at least one year" (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). Article 68(12) requires that "the electronic health data within the secure processing environment shall be deleted within six months of the expiry of the data permit". On the broad reading it reaches access logs kept at patient-record granularity, whose entries are themselves data concerning health; on a narrower reading it reaches only the permitted dataset. The reading is contestable, and Chapter 4, Section 4.6 sets out the grounds on both sides. On the broad reading, for logs kept at patient-record granularity, this is the audit-retention versus erasure tension in its purest form. It is not a conflict assembled across instruments of differing legal weight, but a floor and a ceiling stated in adjacent articles of the same chapter of the same regulation. The implementing acts that might reconcile them are not due from the Commission until 26 March 2027 under Article 73(5), two years before the provisions themselves become applicable on 26 March 2029.

The standard practitioner heuristic for conflicting retention obligations cannot resolve this. Guidance for multi-framework environments routinely advises applying the longest applicable retention period across all frameworks: a rule that works when every obligation is a floor, and fails entirely when one obligation is a ceiling, since extending retention to satisfy the longer floor is precisely what the ceiling forbids. The absence of an applicable heuristic is one reason the conflict has remained open in practice as well as in the literature.

The renumbering of these obligations between the 2022 proposal and the adopted text, from Articles 46 and 50 to Articles 68 and 73, is one reason the commentary has treated them apart (Chapter 1, Section 1.4).

In the cross-border pathway the conflict takes the form Chapter 1, Section 1.2 describes: a granted erasure request leaves an audit record pointing at data that no longer exist, and a refused one must be justified as necessary for a specific legal obligation, as the European Data Protection Board recommended in February 2026 (Matheson, 2026). Practitioner and supervisory commentary reads the erasure obligation as extending to all storage locations of the personal data, backups and logs included (see, e.g., the Norwegian legal commentary summarised in Berngaard, 2024), which is why the audit trail cannot be carved out of the conflict.

The most direct legal objection to the existence of the conflict must be addressed before its technical treatment. Article 17(3)(b) GDPR provides that the right to erasure does not apply to the extent that processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject. On a broad reading, the audit-retention obligation of the eHDSI baseline would simply disapply Article 17 for audit records, and the three regimes would never collide. This thesis argues that the exemption narrows the conflict without dissolving it, for three reasons grounded in the primary instruments. First, the exemption is bounded by necessity: it excuses only the processing that the legal obligation actually requires, and the storage-limitation and data-minimisation principles of Article 5(1)(c) and 5(1)(e) GDPR continue to apply to whatever is retained. The accountability function of a cross-border audit record, namely who accessed what, when, from where, under which legal basis, and with what outcome, does not require continued controller access to the full personal-data payload of the underlying exchange. Retention of the payload in accessible plaintext for the length of the floor therefore exceeds the necessity that Article 17(3)(b) protects. Second, the Norwegian Data Protection Authority's internal-control guidance places precisely this balancing on the controller. It requires organisations to determine lawful retention periods and deletion deadlines through an explicit weighing of purpose, business need, and both retention rights and retention duties imposed by other law (Datatilsynet, n.d.-a). That formulation presupposes, rather than dissolves, the coexistence of retention obligations and deletion obligations on the same records. Third, the six-month deletion ceiling of Article 68(12) EHDS, as operationalised in the TEHDAS2 guidance for Health Data Access Bodies, contains no exemption parallel to Article 17(3)(b); within the secondary-use pathway the ceiling binds regardless of how the erasure-versus-retention balance is struck for the audit floor. The conflict therefore survives the exemption in a precise, narrowed form: the contested object is not whether audit records may be retained, but how much of a record may remain accessible, for how long, and in what state: exactly the question the mechanism developed in Chapter 4 formalises.

A direct technical solution to this tension exists in the literature on cryptographic erasure, though its application to the cross-border health audit problem has not been systematically developed. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's Special Publication 800-88 Revision 2 recognises cryptographic erasure as a purge-grade sanitization technique, in which the encryption key used to protect the data is irreversibly destroyed while the encrypted data remain on the storage medium (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2025). Practitioner implementation guidance asserts that cryptographic erasure is increasingly accepted as a means of satisfying Article 17 in distributed cloud environments, conditional on auditable key management and demonstrably irreversible key destruction (Dhandala, 2026). That assertion is reported here as evidence of industry practice rather than as a statement of regulatory position; the authoritative position on cryptographic unrecoverability is the European Data Protection Board's, set out later in this section. SecuPi (2025) criticises it as operationally costly in row-level encryption; Chapter 6, Section 6.4 engages that critique.

Regulatory doctrine on what counts as erasure has moved substantially during the review period, in a direction that bears directly on this thesis. The Norwegian Data Protection Authority's privacy-by-design guidance states that anonymisation is equated with deletion of personal data ("anonymisering likestilles med sletting av personopplysninger"), so that rendering data irreversibly non-identifiable discharges the deletion obligation (Datatilsynet, n.d.-b). The same authority's anonymisation guidance, however, sets a deliberately high threshold: encryption is characterised as a means of protecting data, not of rendering it unidentifiable, and data remain personal as long as the controller retains any key or linkage capable of re-identification (Datatilsynet, 2015). At Union level, the European Data Protection Board's Guidelines 02/2025 on the processing of personal data through blockchain technologies accept that, where physical deletion is not technically possible, deleting the decryption key can render the data unintelligible and so serve the erasure obligation (European Data Protection Board, 2026b). The Guidelines maintain that encrypted and hashed data may themselves remain personal data, and that the unintelligibility holds at least until the algorithm is broken. The further condition that the controller document the threat model under which the data are considered unrecoverable is not the Board's formulation but Aikebaier's (2026) interpretation, and the two carry different authority. Read together, these positions define the legal corridor within which any cryptographic-erasure mechanism must operate: key destruction can discharge the erasure obligation only if it is irreversible in the documented threat model, and any residue that survives, including hashes and commitments, must itself be assessed against the high anonymity threshold. Chapter 4 designs to this corridor explicitly, and Section 2.4 returns to the residue question.

What does not yet exist in the literature, and what this thesis addresses, is the specific design of a verifiable invalidation mechanism operating at the cross-border audit-trail boundary. That boundary is defined by ATNA, the cross-border retention floor, GDPR Article 17(3)(b), and the six-month rule of Article 68(12). The mechanism must be both technically demonstrable and compatible with the operational reality of Norwegian NCPeH and Helsedataservice. The next section reviews the cryptographic literature that informs the design of such a mechanism.

## 2.4 Cryptographic Approaches to Audit Compliance

A growing body of cryptographic and systems research has developed mechanisms that address parts of the audit-compliance problem in cross-border and multi-institutional health data settings. The four most relevant strands are trusted-execution-environment-based architectures applied to OpenNCP, blockchain-based audit logging adapted to healthcare, zero-knowledge proofs applied to electronic health records and decentralised identity, and the underlying cryptographic primitives of commitment schemes and accumulators that make revocation mathematically tractable. This section reviews each strand in turn, together with the secure-deletion foundation on which cryptographic erasure rests and the adjacent literatures from which the present problem must be distinguished.

The KONFIDO project, funded under the Horizon 2020 programme between 2016 and 2019, remains the most directly relevant architectural ancestor for the present work. KONFIDO extended the OpenNCP cross-border health data exchange platform with a holistic security toolkit organised around four technical pillars. These were photonic Physical Unclonable Functions for hardware-rooted identity, homomorphic encryption for computation on encrypted patient summaries, a Security Information and Event Management system for distributed monitoring, and a blockchain-based auditing component for cross-border traceability and liability support (Diamantopoulos et al., 2019; Staffa et al., 2018). KONFIDO was piloted in Denmark, Italy, and Spain, and the lessons-learned synthesis published by Natsiavas et al. (2021) documented both the technical achievements and the substantive gaps in scaling the approach. Two of those gaps are directly relevant. First, the KONFIDO security architecture relied on Intel Software Guard Extensions trusted execution environments, creating a hardware dependency that complicates broad NCPeH deployment across heterogeneous Member State infrastructures. Second, KONFIDO's blockchain auditing component, designated as a "disruptive logging" mechanism in the project documentation, provided immutable traceability but did not engage the General Data Protection Regulation's right to erasure as a design constraint. The audit chain was treated as a feature whose value derived from its immutability, with the conflict between immutability and erasure left unresolved.

A second strand applies zero-knowledge proofs to electronic health record access control. The closest comparator is Albalwy (2026), whose ZK-EHR framework uses a Groth16 zk-SNARK circuit to let a patient, a doctor or a researcher prove that they hold policy-compliant credentials for cross-institutional sharing without revealing identity, role or affiliation, with encrypted records held off-chain and verification on-chain. It works within a single jurisdiction; the cross-border space addressed by MyHealth@EU, with its eIDAS-based authentication chains and audit repositories spanning several national legal regimes, is distinct.

Health-zkIDM (Bai et al., 2022) shows that decentralised identity management with zero-knowledge components runs at more than 400 transactions per second on Hyperledger Fabric; it targets identity verification rather than audit-trail erasure.

zkFL-Health (Sharma et al., 2025) combines federated learning, trusted execution and succinct proofs generated with Halo2 or Nova, so that an aggregator proves it used exactly the committed client updates, and records the commitments on-chain as what the authors call an "immutable audit trail". It illustrates the pattern on which the mechanism of this thesis also rests: commitments in the trail, data held elsewhere.

Within the Norwegian clinical context, the NewbornTime project provides a deployed precedent. Brunner et al. (2025) document the deployment of a Hyperledger-based digital consent platform at Stavanger University Hospital, used between November 2021 and June 2025 in the collection of 1,012 thermal videos of newborn resuscitation events. The platform stores consent status immutably on-chain while keeping personal data encrypted and off-chain, providing a protected API for authorised retrieval and a documented General Data Protection Regulation-compliant withdrawal mechanism. The clinical viability of blockchain-anchored consent infrastructure in a Norwegian regulated healthcare setting is therefore established, although the system addresses consent recording rather than the audit-trail erasure problem.

A fourth strand of work, less visible in the healthcare literature but foundational to the design of any verifiable invalidation mechanism, is the cryptographic accumulator and commitment scheme literature. Benaloh and de Mare (1994) introduced one-way accumulators as a means of accumulating elements from a finite set into a single short value supporting membership testing. Camenisch and Lysyanskaya (2002) extended this work to dynamic accumulators with efficient revocation of anonymous credentials, providing the mathematical machinery for verifiable membership and non-membership proofs in sets that change over time. Ren (2022) surveys the field, covering applications ranging from anonymous credentials and certificate revocation through to blockchain-based audit systems.

The integrity of the audit trail itself has an older literature of its own. Schneier and Kelsey (1999) designed audit logs in which entries written before an attacker compromises the logging machine can be neither read nor altered without detection, by chaining each entry to its predecessor under evolving keys. Crosby and Wallach (2009) replaced the chain with a Merkle tree (Merkle, 1988), so that the presence of a single entry, and the consistency of an earlier version of the log with a later one, can each be checked with a proof logarithmic in the size of the log. Certificate Transparency standardised that construction for public append-only logs (Laurie, Langley, & Kasper, 2013), and has operated it at the scale of the web's certificate infrastructure (Laurie, 2014). These designs make a log immutable in the sense accountability needs. None was designed for a log that must also lose content on request, and a log that records personal data in its entries, as KONFIDO's blockchain component did, reproduces the immutability-against-erasure tension described above. Chapter 4, Section 4.8 keeps the construction and resolves the tension by deciding what an entry may contain.

A second line of work resolves the same tension the other way, by allowing content inside an integrity-protected structure to change. Chameleon hashes are collision-resistant except for the holder of a trapdoor, who can find collisions at will (Krawczyk & Rabin, 1998). Sanitizable signatures let a designated sanitizer replace marked parts of a signed message while the signature still verifies (Ateniese, Chou, de Medeiros, & Tsudik, 2005), and redactable signatures let parts be removed without any key (Johnson, Molnar, Song, & Wagner, 2002). Redactable blockchains link blocks with chameleon hashes so that a trapdoor holder can replace or remove a block's content and the chain still validates, with the right to be forgotten named as a use case (Ateniese, Magri, Venturi, & Andrade, 2017); later designs spread the power to rewrite across attribute-key holders (Derler, Samelin, Slamanig, & Striecks, 2019) or a voting majority (Deuber, Magri, & Thyagarajan, 2019), and the approaches have been surveyed with the GDPR as their main motivation (Politou, Casino, Alepis, & Patsakis, 2021). Each weakens the binding: integrity comes to mean consistent with what the authorised editor allows rather than unchanged since written, and whoever holds the trapdoor, the sanitizer key, the attribute keys or the majority can rewrite history, in the trapdoor schemes invisibly to anyone without an old copy. The mechanism of this thesis takes the opposite route. Its log is never edited; erasable content is kept out of it, and destroyed by destroying keys. The trust that redaction places in an editor is placed instead in key destruction (Chapter 6, Section 6.3).

The technique this thesis applies sits within a mature secure-deletion literature whose central caution must be carried into the design. The canonical systematisation by Reardon, Basin, and Capkun (2013) defines secure deletion as rendering data irrecoverable from a physical medium, organises approaches by their interface to that medium, and demonstrates that the further an approach operates from the medium, the harder it becomes to assert that destruction has actually occurred. Cryptographic erasure is the limiting case of this observation, and an old one: Boneh and Lipton (1996) proposed making files removable from backups by encrypting them and later forgetting the key. It operates entirely above the medium, reducing the deletion of arbitrarily large data to the deletion of a key, and thereby inheriting in concentrated form the problem that key destruction itself cannot be proven to an external party by inspection. This limitation is not fatal. NIST SP 800-88 Revision 2 recognises cryptographic erase as a purge-grade sanitization technique precisely because, within a documented key-management boundary, it offers high assurance. But it defines the boundary of any crypto-erasure guarantee: the mechanism's properties are conditional on honest and verifiable key-lifecycle management, a condition examined further in Chapter 6.

During the review-update period, the formal treatment of cryptographic erasure advanced significantly. Aikebaier's (2026) systematisation of cryptographic erasure on public ledgers organises the application-layer construction space along two axes, data locus and key custody, into a twelve-cell grid of canonical constructions from 2006 to 2026. It extends IND-CCA security with a key-lifecycle adversary model parametrised by hardware-security-module side-channel leakage, coercion fraction over the custody committee, and algorithmic-break horizon. It then establishes an equivalence proposition between the resulting Destruction-IND notion and the EDPB Guidelines 02/2025 criterion of rendering data unrecoverable. The work is a single-author preprint with industry affiliation and has not been peer-reviewed; it is treated here as a systematisation of the field rather than as settled result, and the thesis does not rest any legal conclusion on it.

Three features of that work matter for the positioning of this thesis. First, it demonstrates that the erasure side of the problem, under what cryptographic conditions key destruction constitutes erasure, now has a rigorous formal treatment on which this thesis can build rather than a gap it must fill. Second, the work confines itself to erasure under Article 17 and does not treat retention under sectoral law. Third, and most directly, among the five open problems it identifies for the field is *erasure under multi-regime retention conflict*: the problem this thesis formalises and resolves. The gap claimed in Section 2.9 is therefore not established solely by this author's failure to find prior work; it is named as open by the most recent systematisation of the relevant cryptographic literature.

Two adjacent bodies of work must finally be distinguished from the problem addressed here, one academic and one practitioner. The academic body is the extensive blockchain-and-GDPR literature. Finck's analyses for the European Parliament and in the data-protection literature established the canonical tension between ledger immutability and the right to erasure. They also produced two findings of continuing relevance: that the meaning of "erasure" and "anonymisation" under GDPR is itself legally uncertain, and that hashed or encrypted data likely remain personal data for GDPR purposes so long as re-identification is possible (Finck, 2018; Finck, 2019). A systematic review of 114 papers in this literature reports difficulties in exercising data-subject rights, the right to be forgotten foremost among them, as the first of three principal issue types arising from blockchain immutability (Belen-Saglam, Altuncu, Lu, & Li, 2023). That conflict is adjacent to, but distinct from, the one this thesis addresses: it arises from a technological property (immutability) chosen by the system designer, whereas the conflict here arises from regulatory instruments imposing simultaneous floors and ceilings on retention regardless of storage technology. The distinction matters for design: a blockchain-immutability conflict can be dissolved by choosing mutable storage, while the tri-lateral regulatory conflict cannot be dissolved by any storage choice at all. Finck's finding on hashes, however, applies with full force to this thesis's mechanism: a commitment that survives invalidation is a residue that must be assessed against the anonymity threshold, and Chapter 4 therefore specifies hiding (salted) commitments and treats the residue question as a first-class design constraint rather than a footnote.

A third adjacent body is the closest prior art to the mechanism proposed in this thesis. A research programme at DePaul University has addressed the reconciliation of retention and purging obligations in relational database systems across a sequence of publications and a granted patent. Scope, Rasin, Lenard, Heart, and Wagner (2022) formalise data-policy compliance rules for retention and purging and implement automated enforcement in a relational database. Companion work extends the approach to database backups through cryptographic erasure (Scope, Rasin, Lenard, Wagner, & Heart, 2022). The approach is claimed in United States Patent 12,425,209 (Scope & Rasin, 2025), which specifies shadow tables holding encrypted copies whose encryption keys are selected according to the purge date of the data they protect, with purging effected by destroying the corresponding key.

This is a substantial body of work and it converges independently on the same insight as the present thesis: that key-lifecycle control is the natural engineering response to conflicting retention obligations. Three differences bound the overlap, and the first is decisive.

The mechanism satisfies the retention obligation by keeping *recoverable* encrypted copies, the key still exists until the purge date arrives, in shadow tables and shadow backups. That is the correct design when both obligations are floors and the purge date is known in advance, because retention simply runs until the later date. It cannot work against a deletion ceiling. Keyed ciphertext remains personal data under the Norwegian threshold established in Section 2.3, since the controller retains the means of re-identification; holding recoverable copies to satisfy a retention floor is therefore precisely what Article 68(12) forbids of a Secure Processing Environment. The tri-lateral case inverts the design requirement rather than extending it.

Second, the work addresses per-value data within a database and its backups, not the audit trail of a cross-border exchange, and it supplies no verifiability semantics: nothing in the framework allows an external auditor to establish what was purged, when, or on what legal ground. Third, the erasure trigger in that work is a scheduled purge date fixed in advance, whereas the erasure right modelled here arrives at an arbitrary time chosen by the data subject, which is what makes the feasibility question non-trivial. The programme is prior art for the technique and for the retention–purge reconciliation problem in databases; it is not prior art for the tri-lateral conflict, for the audit-trail setting, or for the verifiability semantics.

A fourth body concerns whether erasure can be proven at all. Proof-of-Secure-Erasure protocols seek evidence that a prover has erased its memory, and recent formal work relaxes the isolation assumptions those protocols traditionally require (Bursuc, Gil-Pons, Mauw, & Trujillo-Rasua, 2024). In the quantum setting, encryption with certified deletion produces a deletion certificate, and its authors identify public verifiability, allowing parties other than the originator to verify such a certificate, as an open problem (Broadbent & Islam, 2020). This literature marks the outer boundary of the present thesis: the mechanism specified in Chapter 4 logs an *attested* invalidation, not a *proven* one, and the difference between those is the subject of an active research programme rather than something this thesis resolves.

The practitioner body is the rapidly consolidating industry pattern of crypto-shredding for reconciling erasure with record-keeping obligations, articulated most fully for the financial sector, where GDPR Article 17 collides with the five-to-seven-year retention obligations of MiFID II Article 16(7) and analogous instruments (VeritasChain, 2026). The pattern (encrypt per subject, destroy the key on request, retain the ciphertext and the structural record) is the same family of technique this thesis employs, and its cross-sector articulation confirms that the technique itself is established practice rather than a novel contribution. Three things distinguish the present work from this practitioner pattern. First, the financial formulation is bi-lateral (a retention floor against an erasure right); the EHDS setting adds a third, structurally different constraint, a deletion ceiling on Secure Processing Environments, producing a tri-lateral system whose joint satisfiability is not obvious and has not been analysed. Second, the practitioner pattern asserts reconciliation; it does not provide a formal model within which reconciliation is derived, nor verifiability semantics, commitments and a tamper-evident log, by which an external auditor can check that invalidation occurred and what it invalidated. Third, no articulation of the pattern addresses the cross-border health audit trail, its standards stack, or a national implementation substrate. These three deltas (the formal tri-lateral model, the verifiability semantics, and the cross-border health instantiation) are where the contribution of this thesis lies, and Section 2.9 states them as gaps with this precision.

Three observations emerge from this survey. First, the technical components required for a verifiable cryptographic-erasure mechanism for cross-border health audit trails exist in mature form. They include trusted execution environments at the OpenNCP layer, as in KONFIDO (Natsiavas et al., 2021). They include zero-knowledge proof systems applied to healthcare, as in ZK-EHR, Health-zkIDM, and zkFL-Health (Albalwy, 2026; Bai et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2025). And they include blockchain-anchored consent and audit infrastructures with Norwegian clinical validation, as in NewbornTime (Brunner et al., 2025). They also include cryptographic accumulators with revocation semantics (Camenisch & Lysyanskaya, 2002) and the literature that followed, and a formally grounded cryptographic-erasure foundation. Second, none of these works combines these components to address the specific tension between eHDSI-mandated audit retention, GDPR Article 17 erasure rights, and TEHDAS2 Secure Processing Environment deletion in the cross-border European context. Third, the broader policy literature on privacy-enhancing technologies under the European Health Data Space explicitly identifies this kind of governance-and-technology integration as an open research category. The contribution of this thesis is positioned at that named gap.

## 2.5 Formal Approaches to Regulatory-Compliance Verification

The thesis's analytical method belongs to a research tradition that treats legal compliance as a formal-verification problem, and reviewing it both anchors the method and bounds the novelty claim.

The most direct methodological ancestor is the work of Feng, Marsso, Sabetzadeh, and Chechik (2023) on bounded satisfiability checking of legal properties expressed in Metric First-Order Temporal Logic. Their approach formalises obligations drawn from legal texts as temporal-logic properties, reduces satisfiability checking to Satisfiability Modulo Theories solving, and generates counterexamples that witness compliance violations, with case studies spanning regulated domains including healthcare. The significance for this thesis is double-edged. On one side, the general method (regulatory obligations as formal constraints, compliance as a satisfiability question, violations as derivable counterexamples) is established, and this thesis claims no novelty for it. On the other side, the method has not been applied to the specific tri-lateral conflict of the cross-border health audit trail. The instantiation developed in Chapter 4 also employs lighter machinery than the MFOTL-and-SMT apparatus: a feasibility formulation over discretised time with one binary accessibility variable per record, in the constraint-modelling style of the operations-research tradition. The choice of lighter machinery is methodological, not accidental. The tri-lateral conflict turns on a single semantic question, what "verifiable" means for an audit record. A formulation that isolates that question is more defensible, more communicable, and more faithful to the logistics-modelling identity of the thesis than a fully general temporal-logic encoding.

A second relevant strand is the formal treatment of erasure obligations in adjacent technical settings. In machine learning, formal accounts of the right to erasure have produced the machine-unlearning literature; these works formalise what it means to remove a data subject's influence from a trained model, a different erasure object from the audit record treated here, but methodologically kindred in deriving mechanisms from formally stated erasure requirements. In databases, Chakraborty et al. (2025) start from the observation that also motivates this thesis: data regulations such as the GDPR require erasure while leaving its definition open to interpretation. They give a formal definition under which any inference about deleted data, drawn through dependencies between records, stays bounded to what could have been inferred before the data were inserted, and they design mechanisms that enforce it at minimal cost. Their object differs from the one treated here, being erasure inside a single database whose records depend on one another rather than the joint satisfiability of conflicting retention regimes over a record's life. The move is nonetheless kindred, and it corroborates, from peer-reviewed work, that what counts as erasure is a design parameter to be fixed formally rather than a given. In cryptography, the 2026 systematisation of cryptographic erasure discussed in Section 2.4 provides the formal foundation for the erasure side of the present problem, including a security notion and an equivalence argument to the regulatory unrecoverability test, while explicitly excluding sectoral retention law from its scope.

A third strand applies optimisation and operations-research methods to regulatory compliance itself. Mixed-integer and simulation models have been developed for supply-chain compliance under regulatory constraint sets, and compliance-management systems for cross-border data transfer have been proposed as engineering artefacts (Zhuang et al., 2024). This strand demonstrates that compliance reconciliation is a recognised object for the modelling tradition in which the author is trained; it has not, however, been applied to the reconciliation of data-retention regimes.

The synthesis of this section is a precise statement of what is and is not available. Available: a general formal method for legal-compliance satisfiability (Feng et al., 2023); a formal foundation for cryptographic erasure as Article 17 erasure (Aikebaier, 2026; European Data Protection Board, 2026b); optimisation methods for regulatory compliance in physical supply chains. Three things are not available anywhere in the reviewed literature. The first is a formal feasibility analysis of the tri-lateral retention system, with floor, right, and ceiling operating concurrently on the same records, in any sector. The second is any formal treatment situated at the cross-border health audit boundary, and the third is any treatment in information-logistics terms. The Tri-Lateral Retention Feasibility Model of Chapter 4 is constructed to occupy exactly this space, borrowing its method from the first strand, its erasure semantics from the second, and its modelling style from the third.

## 2.6 Norwegian Secondary-Use Infrastructure

Norway's secondary-use infrastructure for health data is the substrate against which the European Health Data Space will be implemented at the national level. The infrastructure has three layers: a governance and access-decision layer through Helsedataservice, a data-custodian layer through the major registry holders, and an analytical-processing layer through a set of Secure Processing Environments coordinated through the NORTRE collaboration but extending, in operational practice, well beyond it. Each layer presents a distinct compliance surface, and each interacts directly with the audit-retention versus erasure tension developed in Section 2.3.

Helsedataservice is the national body through which access to health data for secondary use is decided, and is the presumptive Health Data Access Body for the purposes of the European Health Data Space. Formal designation has not yet occurred. Respondents to the 2025 national hearing on Secure Processing Environment criteria observed that Norway had not at that point appointed a coordinating Health Data Access Body of the kind Articles 55 to 59 EHDS require (Norsk forening for allmennmedisin, 2025). That is consistent with the secondary-use provisions of the Regulation not becoming applicable until 26 March 2029. It took on its current authority on 15 March 2023, when decision-making authority over data access from eleven central health registries was transferred to Helsedataservice under the statutory changes introduced through Prop. 63 L (Forskningsetikk, 2023; Helse- og omsorgsdepartementet, 2020). Applications are submitted through the helsedata.no portal. Helsedataservice registers each application and forwards it to the relevant data custodian, such as the Norwegian Institute of Public Health, the Norwegian Directorate of Health, or the Cancer Registry, and the custodian decides on access. A two-stage cost structure mirrors this two-stage decision structure (Helsedataservice, n.d.).

The Secure Processing Environment layer is where the operational reconciliation of EHDS Article 73 with Norwegian implementation must occur, and the national policy process has now completed its first full cycle there. The SPUHiN project's November 2024 draft recommendations documented the state of Norwegian SPE practice in a passage that remains the decisive empirical fact for mechanism design in this thesis. Based on applicants' own descriptions in permit applications to Helsedataservice during 2023, the drafting team observed that "it is however most common to use either an analysis infrastructure currently available from the universities... or some type of server, database, folder, area or zone most likely provided by the data controller's own IT provider. A few also refer to OneDrive, Office365, disconnected PC or a partner in another country" (SPUHiN, 2024, p. 4). The Norwegian SPE landscape is therefore not merely distributed across the three NORTRE university nodes but operationally fragmented, with substantial secondary-use processing occurring outside any trusted-research-environment substrate.

Following a public hearing conducted between July and October 2025, the criteria were, by the Directorate's account, included in SPUHiN deliverable D5.2, *Recommendations for minimum requirements for Secure Processing Environments in Norway*. The deliverable comprises separate compliance checklists for SPE providers and SPE user organisations, with each requirement graded as mandatory or recommended. It was developed in collaboration with researchers, data custodians, technology suppliers, and authorities, and is positioned as a national reference basis for actors preparing for the coming EHDS requirements pending the Union implementing act (Helsedirektoratet, 2025; SPUHiN, 2025). Inspection of the checklists in the hearing version shows their scope to be information-security management, identity assurance, and access governance. They grade an ISMS requirement as mandatory and ISO 27001 certification as recommended, require eID-based login with defined assurance levels, and set personnel-security controls. Several recommended requirements are explicitly anticipated to harden into expectations as EHDS is implemented (SPUHiN, 2025). What those criteria do not contain, at any grade, is a requirement or mechanism addressing the reconciliation of audit-retention obligations with erasure and deletion obligations on the records the SPE generates. The gap identified by this thesis therefore survives the hearing version, not only the 2024 draft: Norway now has national SPE criteria that govern how the environment is secured and who may enter it, while leaving unaddressed what happens when the retention regimes on its records collide.

The three primary NORTRE nodes (TSD at the University of Oslo (operational since 2012), SAFE at the University of Bergen (2015), and HUNT Cloud at NTNU (2013)) provide infrastructure meeting European trusted-research-environment standards and participate in the EOSC-ENTRUST federation blueprint (Norwegian Trusted Research Environments, n.d.). On the primary-use side, the national infrastructure has moved from preparation into operation during the review period: with the NCPeH pilot of Section 2.2 in production (Helsedirektoratet, 2026), the cross-border audit records this thesis concerns are beginning to be generated in Norway, at presently small volume.

The design consequence carried into Chapter 4 is unchanged and strengthened. A verifiable cryptographic-erasure mechanism that assumes a clean, homogeneous SPE substrate would fail against the documented fragmentation; a mechanism that assumes the substrate question will be settled by the national criteria would fail against their actual scope. The same holds at Union level. The Secure Processing Environment specification published by TEHDAS2 during 2026 as deliverable D7.4 defines the minimum capabilities such environments must offer and the interoperability baseline for federating them. The Joint Action's guidance on using data within such an environment treats deletion at permit closure as an operational service. Neither connects that deletion obligation to the one-year identifiable-log retention floor imposed on the same environment by Article 73(1)(e) (TEHDAS2, 2025, 2026). The body drafting the specifications for both provisions has not addressed their interaction. A mechanism designed at the audit-trail boundary (operating identically whether the processing occurred in TSD, on a controller's own server, or on a disconnected PC, and requiring of the substrate nothing that the national criteria do not already demand) fits the Norwegian context as it actually is.

## 2.7 Information Logistics Framing

The thesis frames the audit-retention versus erasure problem as one of information logistics, which places it within the discipline of Sustainable Energy Logistics at HiMolde and within a literature on cross-border data movement under regulatory constraint.

The International Chamber of Commerce (2025) policy brief on data flows in supply chains documents that cross-border data flows are functionally equivalent to material flows in their structural role within global supply chain operations. They enable real-time coordination, traceability, customs clearance, and the deployment of digital tools across jurisdictional boundaries. They are also subject to the same kind of fragmented regulatory landscape that has historically constrained physical cross-border logistics through divergent national rules. Restrictive and fragmented data policies act as non-tariff barriers in the same operational sense that documentation requirements, retention obligations, and audit rules act as non-tariff barriers in physical logistics.

A Norwegian patient summary moving from a Norwegian National Contact Point for eHealth to a Spanish point of care is a digital good crossing a jurisdictional boundary. It must satisfy the originating jurisdiction's audit and retention rules, the destination jurisdiction's clinical and consent rules, and any superordinate Union-level provisions. The audit log of the cross-border access event is the digital equivalent of the customs documentation that accompanies physical goods. The retention floor on that audit log is the digital equivalent of customs record-keeping. The Union Customs Code requires the person concerned to keep the documents and information on which a customs declaration rests "for at least three years", the period running from the end of the year in which the declaration is accepted or the procedure discharged, and extended while a correction, an appeal or court proceedings are pending (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2013, Article 51). The erasure obligation of Article 17 GDPR has its physical counterpart in destruction: customs authorities "may require goods which have been presented to customs to be destroyed" (Article 197). The two do not collide, because destruction reaches the goods and record-keeping reaches the documents, so the record of a destroyed consignment, including the fact of its destruction, is kept for the full period. That separation of the record from the thing recorded is what Semantics II does for the audit trail, where accountability attaches to the record of the access and of its payload's invalidation rather than to the payload's continued existence (Chapter 4, Section 4.5). Checked against the anchoring result of Chapter 6, Section 6.10, the customs case confirms it from the other side: the destruction power is anchored to an external event, a customs decision, and would cross the record-keeping floor if it reached the documents; it does not, because it is confined to the goods. The analogy also marks where it stops. In customs the floor and the destruction act on different objects by design, whereas in an audit trail the personal data and the record of their processing begin in the same record, and the mechanism has to separate them.

The analogy constrains one decision in the design. Customs record-keeping obligations are not uniform across jurisdictions: a consignment crossing several borders accumulates documentation duties of differing lengths, and the operational answer has never been to build a separate ledger per jurisdiction but to hold one chain-of-custody record whose retention parameter is set per consignment by whichever regime governs it. The same structure is what Chapter 4, Section 4.3 adopts when it makes the retention floor a parameter of the record rather than a constant of the model. That decision is what allows the ten-year cross-border audit floor and the one-year Secure Processing Environment log floor to be instances of one constraint rather than two, and it is what allows a national floor to be substituted without reconstructing the model. It was taken because the logistics reading of the problem made a per-consignment parameter the obvious form, where a purely legal reading would more naturally have produced two separate obligations modelled separately.

A small but technically substantial literature has begun to address cross-border data flow compliance as a logistics and supply chain problem in its own right. Zhuang et al. (2024) propose a Compliance Management System for Cross-Border Data Transfer that integrates multiple legal frameworks into a unified real-time compliance engine, demonstrating that the operational complexity of cross-border data flow under fragmented regulatory regimes is sufficient to warrant a dedicated system-engineering response. The present thesis sits within this emerging literature, contributing a specific mechanism for the audit-retention component of the broader cross-border health data flow problem.

## 2.9 Identified Research Gaps

The literature reviewed in this chapter establishes four specific gaps that motivate the thesis contribution, stated with the boundaries the review imposes.

The first gap is formal-analytical, and it is the primary gap. No published work in any reviewed strand provides a formal model of the tri-lateral retention system, in which an audit-retention floor, a subject-triggered erasure right, and a fixed deletion ceiling operate concurrently on the same records. The strands reviewed are the cross-border health security literature, the cryptographic-erasure literature including its 2026 systematisation, the blockchain-and-GDPR literature, the formal legal-compliance-verification literature, and the practitioner compliance literature. The general method for such modelling exists (Feng et al., 2023), and the erasure side has been formalised (Aikebaier, 2026). The bi-lateral floor-versus-right conflict has been named by regulators (European Data Protection Board, 2026a) and asserted resolved by practitioners (VeritasChain, 2026). But the tri-lateral system, whose deletion ceiling changes the structure of the feasibility question, has not been formally characterised anywhere.

The gap holds also for the conflict's conditional extension. The collision between the six-month deletion ceiling of Article 68(12) and the one-year identifiable-log retention floor of Article 73(1)(e) requires no synthesis across instruments of differing legal weight: on the broad reading, for logs kept at patient-record granularity, it is stated by one regulation, in adjacent articles of one chapter, about one environment. The review conducted for this thesis located no treatment of it in the academic literature or in the professional commentary on EHDS secondary use. The Joint Action drafting the specifications comes closest. Its specification requires deletion at permit expiry "while ensuring compliance with applicable legal obligations and traceability or reproducibility requirements where relevant" and leaves "the deletion and retention policy" to "balance these obligations" (TEHDAS2, 2026, Table 5.2, OPR-6, p. 43). Its expert commentary goes further: log keeping "raises open questions about how accountability and anonymisation obligations intersect", and "further guidance will be needed on retention of project-level collaborative metadata" (Annex 7, p. 96). The body drafting the requirements names the question; no mechanism answering it has been published. Two factors explain the omission without diminishing it. Procedurally, these obligations were renumbered from Articles 46 and 50 of the 2022 proposal on adoption in 2025, so that much existing commentary analyses them under superseded numbering in separate literatures. Substantively, the standard practitioner heuristic for conflicting retention obligations, apply the longest applicable period, is inapplicable where one of the obligations is a ceiling, leaving practitioners without a default to reach for and therefore without an occasion to articulate the conflict. Neither factor makes the conflict any less binding on the environments that must satisfy both provisions from 26 March 2029, nor on the implementing acts that must specify how they are satisfied by 26 March 2027.

The gap does not rest on this author's search alone. Aikebaier's (2026) systematisation of the cryptographic-erasure literature closes by identifying five open problems for the field, of which the fifth is erasure under multi-regime retention conflict. The work is a single-author preprint with industry affiliation and no peer review, and carries correspondingly less weight. What it establishes is that an author surveying this literature in 2026 regarded the problem as open, which is corroborating rather than dispositive. The gap claim rests primarily on the four documented search rounds of Section 2.1.4 and on the absence of any treatment in the regulatory and Joint Action guidance reviewed in Sections 2.3 and 2.6; the preprint is consistent with that finding and does not carry it.

The second gap is application-specific. The cryptographic primitives required — cryptographic erasure as recognised in NIST Special Publication 800-88 Revision 2 and now formally grounded for Article 17 purposes, hiding commitments, and dynamic accumulators with efficient revocation (Camenisch & Lysyanskaya, 2002) — exist in mature form. None has been integrated into a mechanism designed for the cross-border European Health Data Space audit trail, spanning the primary-use NCPeH layer and the secondary-use Helsedataservice and SPE layer. Such a mechanism would need verifiability semantics that let an auditor confirm both that an event occurred and that its payload was lawfully invalidated, demonstrated against synthetic data representative of Norwegian conditions.

The third gap is contextual. The Norwegian SPE criteria in their hearing version (SPUHiN, 2025) govern environment security, identity, and access, and the documented operational reality beneath them remains fragmented to the level of consumer cloud services and disconnected personal computers (SPUHiN, 2024). A compliance mechanism for the retention conflict must therefore be substrate-independent, designed at the audit-trail boundary, and must demand of the processing environment nothing beyond what the national criteria already require: a design constraint no reviewed proposal satisfies.

The fourth gap is legal-technical and was surfaced by the review update. The regulatory corridor for cryptographic erasure has not been mapped onto the audit-trail setting. That corridor comprises the EDPB's conditional acceptance of cryptographic unrecoverability as erasure, the Norwegian equation of anonymisation with deletion, and the simultaneously high Norwegian threshold under which surviving keys, hashes, or linkages keep data personal. In the audit-trail setting, the mechanism's surviving residue (metadata, commitments, invalidation entries) must itself be shown to sit on the lawful side of that threshold. Treating this residue question as a first-class design constraint, rather than an afterthought, is a contribution requirement that the reviewed literature imposes on this thesis and that Chapter 4 discharges.

These four gaps converge on the research question stated in Chapter 1 and are addressed, respectively, by the formal model, the mechanism specification, the boundary-placement design decision, and the residue analysis of Chapter 4, with the demonstration of Chapter 5 witnessing the first two executably.

## 2.10 Conceptual Positioning of the Thesis

The contribution of this thesis is positioned at the intersection of the four identified gaps, and its architecture follows from that positioning. The analytical artefact is the Tri-Lateral Retention Feasibility Model, a constraint formulation in the tradition of formal legal-compliance verification but in the modelling style of operations research. It decomposes each cross-border audit record into event metadata and a committed personal-data payload. It expresses the audit-retention floors, the Article 17 erasure right, and the Article 68(12) deletion ceiling as constraints over discretised time, and examines joint satisfiability under two semantics of audit verifiability. The model is tri-lateral in structure rather than in instrument count: the floor is parameterised, so that the ten-year eHDSI cross-border audit floor and the one-year Article 73(1)(e) Secure Processing Environment log floor are two instances of a single constraint, and jurisdiction-specific floors can be substituted without reconstruction. Under the prevailing plaintext semantics the system is infeasible; under verifiability-as-accountability-preservation it is jointly satisfiable. The mechanism that witnesses this feasibility, Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation, applies the NIST-recognised cryptographic-erase technique at the audit-trail boundary. It operates within the regulatory corridor defined by the EDPB's unrecoverability test and the Norwegian anonymisation-equals-deletion doctrine, with hiding commitments and the invalidation registry supplying the verifiability that the practitioner crypto-shredding pattern lacks (accumulator witnesses being a specified but unimplemented optional layer).

The thesis does not claim novelty for any cryptographic primitive, for the general method of formalising compliance as satisfiability, or for the idea that key destruction can reconcile erasure with retention, each of which the reviewed literature already contains. It claims that three things are together novel: the formal characterisation of the tri-lateral conflict, the expression of that conflict and its resolution in information-logistics terms, and the derivation and demonstration of the reconciling mechanism for the cross-border health audit trail on the documented Norwegian substrate. The comparative analysis of Section 2.8 supports that claim entry by entry. The remaining chapters elaborate this claim through the methodology of Chapter 3, the model and specification of Chapter 4, and the executable demonstration of Chapter 5. The counter-positions identified in this review are engaged substantively in Chapter 6: the unprovability of key destruction, the personal-data status of surviving residues, and the operational-complexity critique of crypto-shredding.
