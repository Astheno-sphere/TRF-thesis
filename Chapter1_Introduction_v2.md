# Chapter 1. Introduction

---

## 1.1 Background and Context

The European Union's health sector is undergoing the most significant data-governance transformation in its history. Regulation (EU) 2025/327 on the European Health Data Space was adopted on 11 February 2025, published in the Official Journal on 5 March 2025, and entered into force on 26 March 2025. It establishes the first Union-wide legal framework for the primary and secondary use of electronic health data across all Member States and European Economic Area countries (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). The regulation creates a binding architecture for cross-border exchange of patient summaries, electronic prescriptions, laboratory results, medical imaging, and hospital discharge reports, with primary-use exchange of patient summaries and ePrescriptions becoming mandatory across the EU and EEA by March 2029, and the remaining priority categories by March 2031 (European Commission, 2025a).

The technical infrastructure underpinning this regulatory shift is MyHealth@EU, the federated pull-based system that allows a clinician in one Member State to retrieve a patient summary or dispense an ePrescription issued in another Member State. Each participating country operates a single National Contact Point for eHealth (NCPeH), and all cross-border exchanges flow through a six-step gateway protocol implemented in the Open National Contact Point (OpenNCP) software stack. These six steps cover service discovery, patient discovery, document retrieval, pivot translation between national clinical document formats and a common European format, security and consent validation, and audit logging (Simjanoska Misheva et al., 2025).

Norway's integration with MyHealth@EU is operational but deliberately narrow: a pilot run by the Norwegian Directorate of Health and the Norwegian Health Network receives patient summaries at two emergency clinics, and no relevant patients had yet presented when the Directorate last reported (Helsedirektoratet, 2026; Chapter 2, Section 2.2 gives the details). As Norway is an EEA member rather than a Member State, EHDS implementation runs through the EEA Joint Committee, and Helsedirektoratet delivered its formal EHDS gap analysis on 11 April 2025 (Helsedirektoratet, n.d.). The cross-border audit records with which this thesis is concerned are therefore beginning to be generated in Norwegian production, on live infrastructure, in small numbers.

Within this regulatory transformation, the most consequential change for clinical practice is the new layer of patient rights established by EHDS. Citizens gain the right to access and download their electronic health data, to view logs of who has accessed it, to set granular restrictions on cross-border sharing, and to opt out of secondary use (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). These rights interact directly with two long-standing instruments. The first is the IHE Audit Trail and Node Authentication (ATNA) integration profile, which defines the technical structure through which access events are logged in cross-border exchanges. ATNA itself prescribes no retention period. The retention obligation arises from the deployment binding: under the eHDSI security framework adopted for MyHealth@EU operations, ATNA-compliant audit repositories are reported to retain their logs for a minimum of ten years (Simjanoska Misheva et al., 2025; IHE International, 2023), a figure this thesis could not confirm in a primary document and treats as illustrative (Chapter 2, Section 2.3). The second is the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, whose Article 17 establishes the data subject's right to obtain erasure of personal data without undue delay under specified conditions (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016).

A third constraint arises from the implementing infrastructure for EHDS secondary use. The TEHDAS2 Joint Action's guideline for Health Data Access Bodies specifies that the electronic health data in a Secure Processing Environment must be deleted within six months of the expiry of the data permit, operationalising Article 68(12) of the EHDS Regulation (TEHDAS2, 2025).

Critically, the same regulation imposes a retention obligation on the same environment. Article 73(1)(e) EHDS requires of every Secure Processing Environment "the keeping of identifiable logs of access to and activities in the secure processing environment for the period necessary to verify and audit all processing operations in that environment; logs of access shall be kept for at least one year" (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2025). Article 68(12) provides that "the electronic health data within the secure processing environment shall be deleted within six months of the expiry of the data permit". Where an environment logs access at patient-record granularity, an entry linking a patient's record to an access is itself electronic health data within the environment. On the broad reading set out in Chapter 4, Section 4.6, the Regulation then requires of a single environment that such data be deleted within six months of permit expiry and that identifiable logs be kept for at least twelve months. That reading is contestable, and the thesis states its grounds rather than assuming it; under the narrower alternative, or where logs are kept at dataset granularity, the conflict in the secondary-use pathway arises through Article 17 erasure requests instead, as it does in the cross-border pathway, and the thesis's results are unaffected.

Cross-border health data exchange therefore operates under three kinds of retention regime that do not yet have a documented technical reconciliation. The first is audit-retention floors, instantiated as the ten-year floor reported for cross-border audit repositories under the eHDSI deployment baseline and the one-year floor imposed on Secure Processing Environment access logs by Article 73(1)(e) EHDS. The second is an erasure obligation for personal data, exercisable at any time by the data subject. The third is a six-month deletion ceiling for processed data in Secure Processing Environments. This thesis is positioned within that unresolved tension.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

The European Health Data Space establishes legal rights and operational obligations, but it does not prescribe how the three retention regimes described above are to be reconciled in technical practice. The result is a structural conflict embedded in the cross-border audit trail.

When a Norwegian patient is treated in another Member State, the access event is logged in the local NCPeH's ATNA-compliant audit repository, retained for the period its deployment binding sets (ten years as reported, Section 1.1), and accessible to the patient through the home country's audit-log interface (Simjanoska Misheva et al., 2025). If, during that window, the patient exercises the right to erasure under GDPR Article 17, the institution faces a choice between three positions, each of which violates one of the other regimes. Deleting the audit record honours Article 17 but destroys the integrity of the audit chain that the eHDSI framework, eIDAS-based access traceability, and EHDS patient rights all depend upon. Retaining the audit record in full preserves the chain but fails the erasure obligation. Pseudonymising the record creates ambiguity about whether the pseudonymisation is reversible, which under GDPR Recital 26 may not satisfy true anonymisation.

An apparent resolution must be confronted at the outset. Article 17(3)(b) GDPR exempts erasure to the extent that processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject. It could therefore be argued that the audit-retention obligation simply disapplies Article 17 for audit records, and that no conflict exists. This thesis takes the position, defended in Sections 2.3 and 4.2, that the conflict survives the exemption in a precise form. The exemption extends only as far as retention is *necessary* for the legal obligation, and the data-minimisation principle of Article 5(1)(c) GDPR requires that no more personal data be retained than the obligation demands. Retaining the full plaintext personal-data payload of an audit record for the length of the floor exceeds what accountability requires, since the accountability function of the record can be preserved without continued access to that payload. Moreover, the six-month deletion ceiling under Article 68(12) EHDS admits no equivalent exemption. What may lawfully be retained is therefore the accountability skeleton of the record rather than its full personal-data content: and specifying, formalising, and demonstrating exactly that residue is the work of this thesis.

The same conflict arises in the secondary-use pathway through erasure requests, and on one reading of Article 68(12) it arises there even without them. Consider a researcher who receives a data permit from the national health data access body, Helsedataservice (Folkehelseinstituttet, 2024), and processes the data within a Norwegian Secure Processing Environment such as those operated by the Trusted Research Environments collaboration NORTRE (Norwegian Trusted Research Environments, n.d.). Article 68(12) EHDS, as operationalised by TEHDAS2, then requires deletion of the electronic health data within the environment within six months of permit expiry. Article 73(1)(e) of the same regulation requires that identifiable access logs for that environment be kept for at least one year. On the broad reading, the environment must therefore be emptied of electronic health data at six months while continuing to hold, for a further six months at minimum, identifiable records of who accessed what within it. No provision resolves the two. Article 73(5) requires the Commission to lay down, by means of implementing acts and by 26 March 2027, the technical, organisational, information-security, confidentiality, data-protection and interoperability requirements for Secure Processing Environments. The secondary-use provisions themselves, Articles 68 and 73 among them, become applicable on 26 March 2029. The collision is therefore not yet operative, and the window in which its resolution could be specified rather than improvised closes with those implementing acts.

These conflicts are not theoretical, and the Norwegian policy process has moved while leaving them unresolved. The SPUHiN project, jointly led by Helsedirektoratet and the Norwegian Institute of Public Health, acknowledged in its November 2024 draft recommendations that Norway lacks a formal regulatory definition of a Secure Processing Environment (SPUHiN, 2024). Following a public hearing between July and October 2025, the Directorate states that the criteria are included in deliverable D5.2, with separate compliance checklists for SPE providers and SPE user organisations, positioned as a national reference basis pending the EU implementing act (SPUHiN, 2025). The version this thesis inspected, the one put to hearing, contains governance and functional requirements but no mechanism reconciling the retention and erasure regimes. The absence is observed in that version and has not been checked against the deliverable as submitted.

A small but rapidly growing body of cryptographic research has begun to address adjacent problems. Albalwy (2026) demonstrates zero-knowledge policy enforcement for cross-institutional health data sharing within a single jurisdiction. Natsiavas et al. (2021) document KONFIDO, the most direct architectural ancestor for cross-border health data security, which combined trusted execution environments, homomorphic encryption, and blockchain-based auditing on the OpenNCP gateway. Bai et al. (2022) describe Health-zkIDM, a Hyperledger Fabric-based decentralised identity system for healthcare. None of these works addresses the audit-retention versus erasure tension at the cross-border audit layer, and none provides a formal account of the conditions under which the three regimes can be jointly satisfied.

The problem this thesis addresses is therefore twofold: the absence of a formal characterisation of the tri-lateral retention conflict in cross-border health data audit trails under EHDS, and the consequent absence of a technically specified, regulation-aligned mechanism for resolving it in a manner compatible with the operational reality of Norwegian implementation.

## 1.3 Research Objectives and Questions

The objective of this thesis is to formally characterise, and then resolve by design, the conflict between the three retention regimes identified in Section 1.2.

The analytical core of the thesis is the Tri-Lateral Retention Feasibility Model (TRF Model), a constraint-satisfaction formulation developed in Chapter 4 within the established tradition of formal legal-compliance verification (Feng, Marsso, Sabetzadeh, & Chechik, 2023). The model decomposes each cross-border audit record into an event-metadata component, itself divided into the accessor's fields and the link to the patient, and a personal-data payload bound by a cryptographic commitment, expresses the three regimes as constraints over discretised time, and examines the satisfiability of the constraint system under two competing semantics of audit verifiability. The model is tri-lateral in its structure, being floor, right, and ceiling, rather than in the number of instruments it accommodates. The retention floor is parameterised, so that the ten-year cross-border audit floor and the one-year Secure Processing Environment log floor are two instances of one constraint rather than two separate constraints. Under plaintext-verifiability semantics, in which a verifiable audit record is one whose payload remains accessible in plaintext, the system is shown to be infeasible whenever an erasure request or permit expiry falls within a record's retention window. Under an alternative semantics, in which verifiability is defined as accountability-preservation (intact metadata, a surviving commitment, and a logged, witnessed invalidation event) the system is shown to be jointly satisfiable for every arrival pattern of erasure requests and permit expiries. The mechanism that realises this feasibility, termed Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation, applies the cryptographic-erasure technique recognised in NIST Special Publication 800-88 Revision 2 at the audit-trail boundary: the key binding a record to its payload is destroyed, while the invalidation event itself is appended to a tamper-evident audit log. The mechanism is thus the constructive witness of the model's feasibility result, not an independently posited design. Two further results locate the conflict: a deletion ceiling collides with a retention floor exactly when it is no longer than the floor, and the event it is anchored to decides whether the collision can be read from the text (Proposition 3); and moving a record out of the regulated environment meets the ceiling but not the right to erasure (Proposition 4).

The thesis is guided by one primary research question.

*Primary research question.* How can a verifiable crypto-erasure mechanism be designed to reconcile the conflicting data-retention requirements of cross-border health data exchange under the European Health Data Space — specifically the tension between the audit-retention floors applying to the exchange, namely the ten-year minimum reported for IHE ATNA audit repositories under the eHDSI deployment baseline and the one-year minimum imposed on Secure Processing Environment access logs by Article 73(1)(e) EHDS, GDPR Article 17's right to erasure, and the six-month Secure Processing Environment deletion ceiling under Article 68(12) EHDS as operationalised by TEHDAS2 — while preserving audit integrity in a Norwegian implementation context?

The primary question is decomposed into three sub-questions.

*SQ1.* What are the precise points of conflict between the audit-retention floors, GDPR Article 17, and the Article 68(12) deletion ceiling in the cross-border EHDS workflow, and under what formal conditions are the three regimes jointly satisfiable?

*SQ2.* Can a cryptographic invalidation mechanism, in which the references binding audit records to personal data are mathematically invalidated rather than the records being deleted, satisfy all three constraints simultaneously, across floors of differing duration?

*SQ3.* How would such a mechanism integrate with Norway's current cross-border infrastructure, specifically the NCPeH operated by Helsedirektoratet and Norsk Helsenett, the Health Data Access Body Helsedataservice, and the Secure Processing Environments operated under NORTRE?

SQ1 is addressed through Chapter 2's literature analysis and the formal characterisation in Chapter 4. SQ2 is addressed through the TRF Model's feasibility propositions, the mechanism specification in Chapter 4, and the executable demonstration in Chapter 5. SQ3 is addressed through Chapter 4's integration analysis and Chapter 6's discussion of implications for Norwegian implementing actors.

## 1.4 Significance and Relevance

The significance of this thesis rests on four convergent factors.

First, the timing is concrete rather than abstract. The Group One priority categories of EHDS become mandatory across the EU and EEA in March 2029, with Group Two following in March 2031, and the implementing acts specifying technical detail are due by March 2027 (European Commission, 2025a). Norway's NCPeH is in live pilot operation, with its outbound capability under construction during 2026 (Helsedirektoratet, 2026), as Section 1.1 describes. Decisions about how cross-border audit trails will handle conflicting retention obligations will be made within the current implementation window. The regulatory background to those decisions is one the European Data Protection Board's February 2026 Coordinated Enforcement Framework report on Article 17, spanning thirty-two supervisory authorities and 764 controllers, explicitly identifies as unsettled, naming the definition and implementation of retention periods among the recurring difficulties (European Data Protection Board, 2026a). In parallel, the Board's Guidelines 02/2025 on blockchain technologies accept, for the first time at Union level, that where physical deletion is not technically possible, deleting the decryption key can render personal data unintelligible and so serve the erasure obligation (European Data Protection Board, 2026b). That acceptance establishes the regulatory corridor within which the mechanism proposed here is designed. The further condition that the controller document its threat model derives from Aikebaier's (2026) interpretation of the Guidelines rather than from the Guidelines themselves.

Second, the Norwegian implementation context provides a concrete, named substrate. It includes a presumptive Health Data Access Body in Helsedataservice (Folkehelseinstituttet, 2024), not yet formally designated under the Regulation, and a developed Trusted Research Environment collaboration in NORTRE (Norwegian Trusted Research Environments, n.d.). It also includes national SPE criteria from the SPUHiN process, whose checklists in the hearing version do not address the retention-erasure question (SPUHiN, 2025), and parallel legislative work on professional confidentiality and cross-organisational access (Stortinget, 2025).

Third, the contribution is positioned at a level the existing literature does not occupy. The general method of formalising legal compliance as a satisfiability problem is established (Feng et al., 2023), and the cryptographic-erasure technique is recognised in standards (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2025) and critiqued in practice. But no published work formally models the specific tri-lateral conflict of the cross-border health audit trail, and no work derives a reconciling mechanism from such a model. This holds also for the conflict's conditional extension. The collision between the six-month deletion ceiling of Article 68(12) and the one-year log-retention floor of Article 73(1)(e) is stated within a single regulation, yet the review conducted for this thesis found no treatment of it in the literature. The Joint Action's specification for these environments comes closest: it requires deletion at permit expiry "while ensuring compliance with applicable legal obligations and traceability or reproducibility requirements", leaves the balance to each operator's policy, and in its expert commentary names "open questions about how accountability and anonymisation obligations intersect" (TEHDAS2, 2026, Table 5.2, OPR-6, p. 43; Annex 7, p. 96). It asks for the balance; it does not supply one. Part of the explanation is procedural: in the 2022 proposal these obligations sat in Articles 46 and 50 and were renumbered on adoption in 2025, so that much existing commentary discusses them under superseded numbering and in separate literatures. The gap is corroborated externally, though the corroborating source carries limited weight and the thesis does not rest the claim on it alone. The most recent systematisation of the cryptographic-erasure literature, a single-author preprint that has not been peer-reviewed, closes by naming erasure under multi-regime retention conflict among five open problems for the field (Aikebaier, 2026). Albalwy (2026) occupies the cross-institutional space within a single jurisdiction; KONFIDO (Natsiavas et al., 2021) addressed cross-border security without resolving the retention-erasure tension and depended on trusted hardware. The thesis's novelty claim is therefore layered and bounded: the formal characterisation of an unformalised conflict, expressed through an information-logistics lens that is itself new to this problem, with the mechanism as the derived solution: an application of recognised primitives, not a claim of foundational cryptographic novelty.

Fourth, the thesis frames the problem as one of information logistics. Physical cross-border logistics meets a similar problem in customs, where documentation must be retained while the goods it describes may be ordered destroyed. This situates the contribution within the disciplinary identity of Sustainable Energy Logistics at HiMolde. The borrowing is specific rather than wholesale: customs law already keeps the record of goods after the goods themselves have been destroyed (Chapter 2, Section 2.7), and that separation of the record from the thing recorded is the move Chapter 4 makes for the audit trail.

## 1.5 Scope and Out-of-Scope

This thesis is bounded by a deliberate and explicit scope, set by what can be defended within the time and resources of an MSc thesis.

*The thesis contributes a formal model, a design-science specification derived from it, and an executable demonstration.* The TRF Model formalises the conflict, and the Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation mechanism is specified as the model's constructive feasibility witness. A Python-based sandbox using synthetic Synthea-generated FHIR data then demonstrates both the infeasibility result under plaintext semantics and the feasibility result under invalidation semantics. The model, the specification, and the demonstration together constitute the artefact.

The thesis explicitly does *not* include the following. First, it does not build a working zero-knowledge SNARK circuit; no component of the mechanism requires one, and the cryptographic machinery that an earlier design iteration envisaged is treated as future work, at the one point where it becomes necessary (Chapter 7, Section 7.5). Second, it does not deploy to a real National Contact Point for eHealth; integration with Norwegian NCPeH operations remains an architectural mapping rather than a runtime test. Third, it does not conduct human-subject interviews, and no qualitative empirical data are reported. Fourth, it does not measure hardware energy consumption, and no sustainability metrics are claimed beyond conceptual framing. Its place in a programme in Sustainable Energy Logistics is therefore in the logistics of information rather than of energy: it treats a regulated cross-border record as a consignment whose custody, retention and destruction are governed by the regimes it crosses. Fifth, it does not use real patient data; all sandbox data are synthetic and contain no information about real individuals. Sixth, it does not implement healthcare-professional authentication or consent-propagation protocols beyond the invalidation mechanism itself. Seventh, it does not provide machine-checked formal proofs; the four propositions are proved informally at the level of rigour standard for design-science work, and their executable check in the sandbox is a demonstration, not a verification in the proof-assistant sense. Eighth, it does not prove destruction of cryptographic keys; the mechanism's guarantees are conditional on honest key destruction, a limitation examined in Chapter 6. Ninth, it does not model the national retention laws of receiving jurisdictions beyond the Union-level instruments; the time horizon of the model is parameterised so that jurisdiction-specific floors can be substituted. Tenth, it does not address non-EU and non-EEA jurisdictions.

Three components related to the broader research direction are outside the scope of this thesis: a zero-knowledge assertion circuit for healthcare-professional authorisation, a sustainability indicator for proof systems, and a cross-border consent-revocation propagation protocol. Results for them have not been produced and are not claimed.

## 1.6 Methodological Approach

The thesis adopts Design Science Research, as established by Hevner, March, Park, and Ram (2004) and elaborated by Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, and Chatterjee (2007), in its specification-and-demonstration variant, extended with a formal-modelling step. The DSR cycle of problem identification, objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication is mapped onto the thesis chapters, with the design phase anchored in the TRF Model. The mechanism is developed as the constructive answer to a formally stated feasibility question, following the tradition of formal compliance verification exemplified by Feng et al. (2023). It employs lighter machinery, a feasibility formulation over one binary state variable per record, suited to defence at Master's level and to the logistics-modelling training of the author's programme.

The project name VERA-SNARK originates from an earlier design iteration, presented in preliminary form at the NORS 2025 conference, which envisaged zero-knowledge succinct arguments as the enforcement machinery. The design-science process (specifically, the formal analysis of what the three retention regimes actually require) showed that cryptographic commitments and a logged invalidation registry suffice for the audit-trail problem, and that no SNARK is required; an accumulator layer is specified in Chapter 4 as optional and is not implemented. The project name is retained for continuity; the mechanism specified and demonstrated in this thesis contains no SNARK, and the evolution from the earlier architecture is itself an instance of the DSR cycle functioning as intended.

The demonstration is conducted in a controlled sandbox using synthetic data generated by Synthea, an open-source generator of synthetic patient records compliant with HL7 FHIR R4, the version reflected in the current Norwegian basisprofiler maintained by HL7 Norway (HL7 Norway, 2025). The sandbox has two layers: a feasibility checker that walks synthetic audit records through randomised arrivals of erasure requests and permit expiries, verifying that the constraint system fails under plaintext-verifiability semantics and holds under invalidation semantics; and a realism layer feeding Synthea-generated records into the checker. The evaluation strategy is analytical: internal validity through the logical consistency of the model and the coherence of mechanism and propositions; external validity through compatibility with verified existing infrastructure; reliability through deterministic execution with fixed random seeds. No empirical performance benchmarks are reported. The full methodological treatment is provided in Chapter 3.

## 1.7 Thesis Structure

The thesis is organised in seven chapters and appendices.

*Chapter 1* introduces the regulatory background, the problem, the research objectives, the significance of the study, the scope and explicit out-of-scope boundaries, and the methodological approach.

*Chapter 2* reviews the literature in six domains: the cross-border health data exchange landscape under EHDS; the audit-retention versus right-to-erasure tension, including the Article 17(3)(b) exemption argument; cryptographic approaches to audit compliance, including secure deletion and tamper-evident logging; formal approaches to regulatory-compliance verification; the Norwegian secondary-use infrastructure; and the information-logistics framing. It identifies the research gaps and positions the contribution.

*Chapter 3* establishes the design science research paradigm, the specification-and-demonstration variant extended with formal modelling, the synthetic data methodology, the sandbox approach, and validity, reliability, and ethical considerations.

*Chapter 4* develops the TRF Model, covering its sets, decomposition, constraints, the two verifiability semantics, the infeasibility and feasibility propositions, and the anchoring proposition. It then specifies the Crypto-Erasure by Invalidation mechanism derived from it, including the key hierarchy, the invalidation registry and the tamper-evident log, analyses its residue, compares it against six rival designs with the location proposition, and traces it requirement by requirement to the regulation, the Joint Action's specification for Secure Processing Environments, and the Norwegian NCPeH and NORTRE infrastructure (Table 4.1).

*Chapter 5* presents the sandbox demonstration: the executable feasibility check, the independent exhaustive cross-check, a bounded SMT check of Propositions 1–3, the audit-log integrity and tamper tests, and the optional run with real envelope encryption. *Chapter 6* discusses findings and their implications for practice, theory, and policy, engages the principal counter-positions, and tests the model on a statute outside health. *Chapter 7* concludes, summarises contributions, revisits limitations, and outlines future work.
